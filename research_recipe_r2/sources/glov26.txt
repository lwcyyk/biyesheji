Abstract
Threshold public-key encryption securely distributes private key shares among multiple participants, requiring a minimum number of them to decrypt messages. We introduce a quantum-resistant threshold public-key encryption scheme based on the code-based Niederreiter cryptosystem that achieves security against chosen ciphertext attacks. A previous attempt was made recently by Takahashi, Hashimoto, and Ogata but we show that it contains a critical security flaw that allow adversaries to exploit malformed ciphertexts to gain information about the secret key. We formalize a generic conversion enhancing security of (classical) public-key encryption from one-wayness against passive attacks to indistinguishability against chosen-ciphertext attacks. The conversion uses a non-interactive zero-knowledge argument with strong security properties to ensure ciphertext well-formedness. We then provide an instantiation for Niederreiter ciphertexts based on recent techniques introduced in the “MPC-in-the-head” paradigm. The publicly verifiable validity of ciphertexts makes this scheme suitable for threshold public-key encryption and prevents an attack similar to the one on Takahashi–Hashimoto–Ogata scheme. Finally, we introduce a field-switching technique that allows to improve the multi-party computation protocol for decryption, reducing the shared secret key size and computational overhead.

Appendix A: Preliminaries
Here is a description of the constant-round multi-party protocols for elementary algebraic operations that we need in our multi-party decoding algorithm. All these protocols can be instanciated in the information theoretical model, without any addition cryptographic assumption (using the BGW protocol [
6
] for example).
FieldInv
: this protocol computes a shared field element
\([a^{-1}]\)
given a shared non-zero field element [
a
] with
\(\mathcal {O}(1)\)
secure multiplications. A version of this protocol for any group is given in [
3
] and can be applied to
\(\mathbb {F}_q^*\)
.
FirstPowers
: this protocol takes a non-negative integer
k
and a shared non-zero field element [
b
] and compute all the powers
\([b^2], [b^3], \dots , [b^k]\)
. Such a protocol is given in [
3
] as a fan-in multiplication of
k
non-zero elements, but can be adapted straightforwardly. It requires
\(\mathcal {O}(k)\)
secure multiplications.
EqTesting
: this protocol aims at proving that a shared element [
a
] is zero. While many solutions exist for this protocol, see [
22
,
53
], the underlying field must be of large characteristic, which is not our case. For our needs, it will be sufficient to reveal publicly the value of
\([ra]=[r]\times [a]\)
for a non-zero random element
r
. This would need
\(\mathcal {O}(1)\)
secure multiplications.
PolyMul
: Mohassel and Franklin proposed in [
50
] a protocol for multiplying two shared polynomials of degree
n
in
\(\mathbb {F}_q[X]\)
that only require
\(\mathcal {O}(n)\)
secure multiplications in
\(\mathbb {F}_q\)
. Note that this protocol enables the multiplication of two elements of a field extension of
\(\mathbb {F}_q\)
of degree
n
with the same complexity, if the irreducible polynomial defining the extension is public.
PolyDiv
: this protocol, also provided in [
50
], allows to compute the quotient and the remainder of two shared polynomials [
f
] and [
g
] with
\(\deg f \ge \deg g\)
using only
\(\mathcal {O}(\deg f)\)
secure multiplications. Note that together with the previous protocol, this enables the multiplication of elements of an extension field that is described with a shared irreducible polynomial.
FastEval
: this protocol aims at computing the evaluations of a shared polynomial of
\(\mathbb {F}_q[X]\)
of degree less than
d
on a set of
d
shared elements of
\(\mathbb {F}_q\)
. The best constant-round solution to date was proposed recently in [
38
]. It needs only
\(\mathcal {O}(\tau \cdot d^{1+1/\tau })\)
shared products and
\(\mathcal {O}(\tau )\)
rounds for any constant parameter
\(\tau \)
.
RatRecon
: given two shared polynomials [
u
] and [
g
], this protocol computes the two shared polynomial [
v
] and [
r
] such that
\(uv = r \mod g\)
, with degree constraints on
r
and
v
. For our need, as pointed out in [
62
], the exact degree of
v
and
r
is known in advance if the error is of correct Hamming weight. Following classical results on gcd and subresultant, see [
35
, Sect. 5.7], this computation can be done through the inversion of a well-chosen subresultant matrix of
u
and
g
. Using a result from [
20
], this can be done with
\(\mathcal {O}(d^3)\)
secure products where
\(d=\deg g\)
.
Appendix B: Proof of Theorem
2
Proof
Let
\(\mathcal {S}\)
be a simulator for
\((\mathcal {P},\mathcal {V})\)
and let
\(\mathcal {T}\)
be a one-time simulation extractor for
\((\mathcal {P},\mathcal {V})\)
. Let
\(\mathcal {A}=(\mathcal {A}_1,\mathcal {A}_2)\)
be an IND-CCA2
\(^+\)
adversary running in time at most
t
against
\((\mathcal {K}',\mathcal {E}',\mathcal {D}')\)
, and making at most
\(q_O\)
queries. For
\(b \in \{0,1\}\)
, we denote by
\({\textbf {Exp}}^{real}(\mathcal {A},b)\)
the game
\({\textbf {Exp}}^{\text {IND-CCA2}^+}(\mathcal {A},b)\)
. First, let us define the game
\({\textbf {Exp}}^\mathcal {S}(\mathcal {A},b)\)
as follows:
The difference between
\({\textbf {Exp}}^{\mathcal {S}}(\mathcal {A},b)\)
and
\({\textbf {Exp}}^{real}(\mathcal {A},b)\)
is that in
\({\textbf {Exp}}^{\mathcal {S}}(\mathcal {A},b)\)
,
\(\mathcal {S}\)
generates the proof corresponding to the challenge ciphertext instead of
\(\mathcal {P}\)
. Let us show that the two games cannot be distinguished with probability greater than
\(\epsilon _{ZK}\)
.
Lemma 2
$$\begin{aligned}\big | \Pr [{\textbf {Exp}}^{\mathcal {S}}(\mathcal {A},b) = 1] - \Pr [{\textbf {Exp}}^{real}(\mathcal {A},b) = 1 ] \big | \le \epsilon _{ZK}.\end{aligned}$$
Proof
From
\(\mathcal {A}= (\mathcal {A}_1,\mathcal {A}_2)\)
, let us build an algorithm
\(\mathcal {A}'\)

\(\mathcal {A}'\)

\(\mathcal {A}'\)

\(\mathcal {A}_1\)

when

\((t_{ZK},\epsilon _{ZK})\)

Lemma 3
For any adversary
\(\mathcal {A}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned}\Pr [{\textbf {bad}}_b] \le \epsilon _s\end{aligned}$$
Proof
This lemma is proved using the one-time simulation straight-line extractability of
\((\mathcal {P},\mathcal {V})\)
(see Definition
7
). Let us build an attacker
\(\mathcal {C}\)
for the game
\({\textbf {Exp}}^{simextract}(\mathcal {C})\)
using
\(\mathcal {A}\)

\(\mathcal {C}\)
works as follows:

\(\mathcal {C}\)

pk
,
sk

\(\mathcal {K}(1^\lambda )\)

\(\mathcal {C}\)

\(\mathcal {A}_1(pk)\)

\(\mathcal {A}_1\)

G

\(\mathcal {C}\)
forwards the query to its oracle, and sends back the answer to
\(\mathcal {A}_1\)

\(\mathcal {C}\)
adds both the query and the answer to a list
\(\mathcal {Q}(\mathcal {A}^G)\)
.
When
\(\mathcal {A}_1\)
queries the decryption oracle on
\((c,\pi )\)

\(\mathcal {C}\)
verifies the proof using
\(\mathcal {V}^G\)
. If the proof is incorrect, it sends back

\(\mathcal {C}\)

\(\mathcal {T}((pk,c),\mathcal {Q}(\mathcal {A}^G))\)

\(({\mathfrak {m}},r)\)

\(\mathcal {E}({\mathfrak {m}},r,pk)=c\)

\(({\mathfrak {m}},r)\)

c

\(\mathcal {C}\)

\(((pk,c),\pi )\)

\(\mathcal {C}\)
sends back
m
as an answer to
\(\mathcal {A}_1\)

2.
When
\(\mathcal {A}_1\)

\(({\mathfrak {m}}_0,{\mathfrak {m}}_1)\)

\(\mathcal {C}\)
samples a random
\(r^*\)
and computes
\(c^*\)
as
\(\mathcal {E}({\mathfrak {m}}_b,r^*,pk)\)

\((pk,c^*)\)
to its challenger, and receives back a simulated proof
\(\pi ^*\)
.
3.
\(\mathcal {C}\)
resumes the execution of
\(\mathcal {A}_2\)
with
\((x^*,\pi ^*)\)
as input. When
\(\mathcal {A}_2\)

Which concludes the proof of Theorem

\(\square \)
Appendix C: Proof of Lemma
1
Proof
If
\(\texttt {wt}(r)=t\)
, let
\(E = \{ \ i \in \llbracket 1,n \rrbracket \ | \ r_i \ne 0 \ \}\)
and
\(Q = \prod _{i \in E} (X - \gamma _i)\)
. Remark that
Q
is of the appropriate degree and monic. Notice that for
\(1 \le i \le n\)
, either
\(r_i = 1\)
and then
\(Q(\gamma _i) = 0\)
, or
\(r_i=0\)
and then
\(S_r(\gamma _i) = r_i = 0\)
. Overall, we have that for
\(1 \le i \le n\)
,
\(S_r(\gamma _i)Q(\gamma _i) = 0\)
By definition of
F
, we therefore have that
F
divides
\(S_rQ\)
, thus there exists
\(P \in \mathbb {F}_{poly}[X]\)
such that
\(S_rQ = FP\)
, and we have
\(\deg P \le \deg S_r + \deg Q - \deg F \le t-1\)
. Moreover, since
\(S_r(\gamma _i) = 0\)
for exactly
\((n-t)\)
of the
\(\gamma _i\)
’s, we have that
\(\deg (\gcd (S_r,F)) = n-t\)
. Using a well-known result on subresultants and the remainder sequence of Euclidean algorithm, see [
35
, Corollary 6.48], this implies that there exists
\(U,V \in \mathbb {F}_{poly}[X]\)
such that
\( (S_rU + FV) \text { quo } X^{n-t} = 1\)
with
\(\deg (U) < \deg (F)-(n-t) = t\)
,
\(\deg (U) < \deg (S_r)-(n-t) = t-1\)
.
Suppose that there exists
\(Q \in \mathbb {F}_{poly}[X]\)
monic of degree
t
, and
\(P,U,V \in \mathbb {F}_{poly}[X]\)
of degree
\(\le t-1\)
,
\(< t\)
and
\(< t-1\)
respectively, such that we have
\(S_rQ = FP \ \text {and} \ (S_rU + FV) \text { quo } X^{n-t} = 1\)
. Then, for
\(1 \le i \le n\)
,
\(S_r(\gamma _i)Q(\gamma _i) = r_iQ(\gamma _i) = F(\gamma _i)P_1(\gamma _i) = 0\)
. Since
Q
is of degree
t
, then
Q
has a most
t
roots among
\(\{\gamma _i\}_{1 \le i \le n}\)
. Using that
\(r_iQ(\gamma _i) = 0\)
for
\(1 \le i \le n\)
, this implies
\(r_i = 0\)
on at least
\(n-t\)
positions, i.e.
\(\texttt {wt}(r) \le t\)
.
In addition,
\((S_rU + FV) \text { quo } X^{n-t} = 1\)
implies that
\(\deg (S_rU + FV) \le n-t\)
. Moreover,
\(\gcd (S_r,F)\)
divides
\(S_rU+FV\)
, thus
\(\deg (\gcd (S_r,F)) \le \deg (S_rU + FV) \le n-t\)
. Since
\(F = \prod _{i=1}^n (X - \gamma _i)\)
, this means that
\(S_r\)
evaluates to 0 on at most
\(n-t\)
elements in
\(\{\gamma _i\}_{1 \le i \le n}\)
. Since
\(\forall 1 \le i \le n, S_r(\gamma _i) = r_i\)
, we have
\(\texttt {wt}(r) \ge t\)
.
Overall, we have
\(\texttt {wt}(r) = t\)
, which is the desired result.
\(\square \)

\(\mathcal {S}\)
simulates a response that is

\({\textbf {ZK}}^{\mathcal {S}}\)

\({\textbf {ZK}}^{real}\)

\(1 \le e \le \tau \)
.
Simulator 0

\(H=( I_{n-k} | T) \in \mathbb {F}_2^{n-k \times n}\)
,

, and

\(\mathcal {H}_1\)
and
\(\mathcal {H}_2\)

\(\mathcal {H}_1\)

\(q_2/2^{2 \lambda }\)

\(\mathcal {H}_2\)

\(\textsf {salt}\)

\(\mathcal {H}_1\)
and
\(\mathcal {H}_2\)

\(2 \lambda \)

\((q_1+q_2)/2^{2 \lambda }\)

\(\textsf {seed}_{i^*}\)

\(\text {TreePRG}(\textsf {salt},\textsf {seed})\)

\(\text {TreePRG}\)

\((t,\epsilon _{PRG})\)
-secure, the output of this simulator is at most

Simulator 3

\(\mathcal {H}_0\)

uniformly at random, and then request
\(\mathcal {H}_0\)
and have the correct values, as in step 5 and 6 of
\(\mathcal {S}\)

\(\textsf {salt}\)

\(\textsf {seed}_{i^{*}}\)
is
\(PRG(\textsf {salt},\textsf {seed}_{i^{*}})\)

PRG
is
\((t,\epsilon _{PRG})\)
-secure, this value is
\(\epsilon _{PRG}\)

\(\tau \cdot \epsilon _{PRG}\)

\(\tau \)

\(\tau \cdot \epsilon _{PRG}\)

\(\tau \cdot \epsilon _{PRG}\)

\(i^* = N\)

\([\alpha _j]_{i^*}\)
,
\([\beta _j]_{i^*}\)
,
\([v_j]_{i^{*}}\)
,
\([{\varvec{\alpha }'}_j]_{i^*}\)

\([{\varvec{\beta }'}_j]_{i^*}\)

Appendix E: Proof of Theorem

Proof
Before proving the theorem, we need to estimate the probability that an incorrect polynomial equation is flagged as correct for a given proof execution.
Lemma 4
For a proof
\(\pi \)
and an execution
\(e \in \llbracket 1, \tau \rrbracket \)
, we say that
\([r_A]^{(e)},[P]^{(e)}\)
,
\([Q]^{(e)},[{\varvec{y}}]^{(e)},[V]^{(e)}\)
form a good witness of the protocol whenever the values
\(r_A^{(e)}\)
,
\(P^{(e)}\)
,
\(Q^{(e)}\)
,
\({\varvec{y}}^{(e)}\)
and
\(V^{(e)}\)
are such that
\(S_r^{(e)}Q^{(e)}= F P^{(e)}\)
and
\((S_r^{(e)}U^{(e)} + FV^{(e)}) \text { quo } X^{n-t} = 1\)
(where
\(S_r^{(e)}\)
is built by interpolation from
\(r_A^{(e)}\)
and
\(U^{(e)}\)
is built from
\({\varvec{y}}^{(e)}\)
). If the values form a bad witness (i.e. not a good witness), the probability of
\((v^{(e)}_j)_{j \in \llbracket 1,s \rrbracket }\)
to be all zeroes and
\(\vec {v}'^{(e)} = \vec {1}\)
is bounded by
\(p=(\frac{n+t}{\Delta })^s\)
, where
\(\Delta =\#\mathbb {F}_{points}\)
and
s
is the number of evaluation point
\(\rho _j\)
, if
\(t-1 \le n\)
.
Proof
We omit the notations related to the execution
e
. If the values form a bad witness, then either
\(S_rQ \ne F P\)
or
\((S_rU + FV) \text { quo } X^{n-t} \ne 1\)
.
If
\(S_rQ \ne FP\)
, let us evaluate the probability that for
\(1 \le j \le s\)
, we have
\(v_j = 0\)
. This can happen only if the product verification fails (this event has probability
\(1/\Delta \)
) or if
\(\rho _j \in \mathbb {F}_{points}\)
(which is sampled uniformly) is a root of
\(S_rQ - FP\)
(i.e.
\(S_rQ(\rho _j) = FP(\rho _j)\)
). Moreover,
\(S_rQ_1 - FP_1\)
is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most
\(n-1+t\)
. Therefore, the probability of
\(\rho _j\)
to be a root of
\(S_rQ - FP\)
is at most
\((n+t-1)/\Delta \)
. Overall, the probability for
\(v_j\)
to be 0 is at most
\((n+t-1)/\Delta + 1/\Delta =(n+t)/\Delta \)
. Over the
s
independent values
\(v_j\)
, the probability that
\((v^{(e)}_j)_{j \in \llbracket 1,s \rrbracket }\)
is all zeroes is upper bounded by
\(((n+t)/\Delta )^s = p\)
.
If
\((S_rU + FV) \text { quo } X^{n-t} \ne 1\)
, then for
\(1 \le j \le s\)
, we have that
\(\vec {v}'_j = 1\)
if
\(\rho _j\)
is root of
\((S_rU + FV) \text { quo } X^{n-t} - 1\)
(which is of degree
\(2t-2\)
), or that the product verification fails (which happens with probability
\(1/\Delta \)
). The probability that
\(\rho _j\)
is a root of this polynomial is at most
\((2t-2)/\Delta \le (n+t-1)/\Delta \)
. Therefore, the probability that
\(\vec {v}'_j = 1\)
is at most
\( (n+t)/\Delta \)
. Over the
s
independent values
\(\vec {v}'_j\)
, the probability that
\(\vec {v}'\)
is
\(\vec {1}\)
is upper bounded by
\(((n+t)/\Delta )^s = p\)
.
In both cases, the probability of a false positive (i.e.
\(v_j= 0\)
for all
j
and
\(\vec {v}' = \vec {1}\)
) for the verification is upper bounded by
p
.
\(\square \)

\((H,c_1,c_2)\)

The first scheme
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I}}\)
directly applies Dodis–Katz conversion [
29
]: it starts from an IND-CCA2 KEM-DEM relying on Classic-McEliece and an IND-CCA2 secret-key encryption scheme. The message is encrypted using the secret-key encryption with an ephemeral secret key
k
divided into
\(\texttt {N}\)
shares. These shares are encrypted with the secret-key encryption using
\(\texttt {N}\)
keys produced by the encapsulation mechanism for the different Classic-McEliece public keys. The set of ciphertexts is signed using a one-time signature. The secret key is just the secret key
sk
of the Classic-McEliece scheme (whose size is
\((n+t)m\)
bits), the ciphertext expansion comes from the
\(\texttt {N}\)
Classic-McEliece ciphertext
\(c_{\textsf {CM}}\)
encapsulating
\(\texttt {N}\)
keys and the encryptions of the shares of the initial secret key
k
, the signature
\(\sigma \)
and the verification key
vk
. The signature can be instantiated from a code-based signature: the authors proposed the one from [
10
] whose size for 128-bit security is 15 kB. The decryption is non-interactive.
The second scheme
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I\!I}}\)
first generically makes a one-way CPA encryption scheme threshold with the same security, and then applies Cong et al.’s techniques [
19
] to transform a (rigid deterministic) threshold one-way CPA secure encryption into a CCA-secure hybrid encryption in the spirit of REACT [
54
]. The first step generates a blow up of all parameters since the number of keys and ciphertext components are in
\(\mathcal {O}(\left( {\begin{array}{c}\texttt {N}-1\\ \texttt {T}\end{array}}\right) )\)
(because it needs encryption to any possible subsets of
\(\texttt {T}-1\)
elements among
\(\texttt {N}\)
) which makes it completely impractical when
\(\texttt {N}\)
grows.
Cong et al.’s conversion is interesting when the considered one-way CPA secure encryption has an efficient distributed decryption. That is why in the last scheme
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I\!I\!I}}\)
, the authors of [
29
] tried to design a distributed decryption for McEliece to remove the dependency in
\(\left( {\begin{array}{c}\texttt {N}-1\\ \texttt {T}\end{array}}\right) \)
. Note that for both
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I\!I\!I}}\)
and
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I\!I}}\)
Cong et al.’s conversion requires the secure distributed evaluation of some hash functions (which counts as the
\(\mathcal {O}(\left( {\begin{array}{c}\texttt {N}\\ \texttt {T-1}\end{array}}\right) n)\)
and the extra
\(\mathcal {O}(n)\)
in the communication complexity).
All the parameterized sizes for secret keys and ciphertexts are recapitulated in Table
2
. Both
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I}}\)
and
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I\!I\!I}}\)
use hashed values that are of length
\(\ell _g\)
and
\(\ell _h\)
. Note that
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I}}\)
and
\(\Pi _{t}^{\texttt {I\!I}}\)
have secret key and ciphertext sizes depending on the number of participants
\(\texttt {N}\)
.
