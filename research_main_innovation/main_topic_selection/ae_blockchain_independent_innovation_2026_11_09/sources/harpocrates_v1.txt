𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates}: Privacy-Preserving and Immutable Audit Log for Sensitive Data Operations
Mohit Bhasi Thazhath, Jan Michalak, Thang Hoang
Affiliation: Department of Computer Science, Virginia Tech, USA
{mohitbt, janmichalak, thanghoang}@vt.edu
Abstract
The audit log is a crucial component to monitor fine-grained operations over sensitive data (e.g., personal, health) for security inspection and assurance.
Since such data operations can be highly sensitive, it is vital to ensure that the audit log achieves not only validity and immutability, but also confidentiality against active threats to standard data regulations (e.g., HIPAA) compliance.
Despite its critical needs, state-of-the-art privacy-preserving audit log schemes (e.g., Ghostor (NSDI ’20), Calypso (VLDB ’19)) do not fully obtain a high level of privacy, integrity, and immutability simultaneously, in which certain information (e.g., user identities) is still leaked in the log.
In this paper, we propose 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates}, a new privacy-preserving and immutable audit log scheme.
𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} permits data store, share, and access operations to be recorded in the audit log without leaking sensitive information (e.g., data identifier, user identity), while permitting the validity of data operations to be publicly verifiable.
𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} makes use of blockchain techniques to achieve immutability and avoid a single point of failure, while cryptographic zero-knowledge proofs are harnessed for confidentiality and public verifiability.
We analyze the security of our proposed technique and prove that it achieves non-malleability and indistinguishability.
We fully implemented 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} and evaluated its performance on a real blockchain system (i.e., Hyperledger Fabric) deployed on a commodity platform (i.e., Amazon EC2).
Experimental results demonstrated that 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} is highly scalable and achieves practical performance.
I Introduction
Remote data storage systems have become predominant in the past decade due to the growth of cloud computing.
Cloud Service Providers (CSPs) have dedicated resources to store data, and have developed a suite of services to support it such as AWS S3, Azure Blobs, GCP Buckets.
The widespread use of cloud data storage has made it easy to share/access remote data.
Given that CSPs generally maintain a vast amount of user data,
many of which can be highly sensitive (e.g., personal, health),
it is vital to maintain an audit log that records all the data operations (e.g., sharing, access activities)
to support system integrity inspection and critical security assurances such as intrusion detection and problem analysis [1].
Many data audit log schemes have been proposed to record fine-grained operations (e.g., read, write, share) over sensitive data (e.g., Electronic Health Records (EHRs) [2, 3], supply chains [4, 5, 6, 7]) in a tamper-resistant and immutable manner.
Despite their merits, achieving the immutability and integrity of the audit log may not be sufficient to ensure security against active threats.
This is because the audit log contains all data operations (i.e., metadata), which can reveal significant sensitive information.
By analyzing the data log, the adversary can obtain the needed information without having to access the actual data [8].
Such metadata leakage has become notorious in related areas such as communication surveillance.
A former NSA General Counsel stated, “Metadata absolutely tells you everything about somebody’s life” [9].
Due to the sensitivity of metadata, it is vital to ensure integrity and confidentiality of audit logs as indicated in standard data regulations such as The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) [10].
To enable both privacy and integrity, several privacy-preserving data audit log schemes have been proposed.
Preliminary constructions permit data operations to be logged in a single server with privacy and integrity guarantees using cryptographic tools such as digital signatures and symmetric encryption [11, 12].
Despite their merits, such centralized approaches suffer from a single point of failure, in which the corrupted server can compromise the validity and confidentiality of the audit log.
To address single point of failure, several decentralized privacy-preserving audit log approaches have been proposed using blockchain techniques [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].
However, there are certain limitations to these techniques.
For example, Droplet [17] offers confidentiality but not anonymity.
Ghostor [18] records data sharing activities with anonymity, but the validity can only be verified privately by the data owner.
This requires all the users to participate in the audit process to verify the validity of the data operations, thereby reducing the audit transparency.
Calypso [19] achieves partial anonymity (i.e., leaks data owner identity) and private verifiability.
Research gaps.
Given that existing data logging techniques lack a certain degree of anonymity, integrity, confidentiality, and transparency, our objective is to design a new privacy-preserving audit log scheme that can offer all desirable security properties for standard data regulations compliance.
Contribution.
In this paper, we propose 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates}, a new privacy-preserving and immutable audit log scheme for sensitive data operations.
𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} permits data operations (i.e., store, share, access) to be recorded in the audit log with validity, confidentiality, and public verifiability guarantees.
𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} makes use of blockchain technologies (e.g., distributed ledger) to achieve immutability and validity, while confidentiality and public verifiability are achieved using advanced cryptographic techniques such as zero-knowledge proofs [20].

𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} achieves the following properties.
•
Record Immutability: All the data operation records cannot be modified by anyone.
•
Full Anonymity: The identity of the data and the user(s) remain hidden all the time. This security guarantee is stronger than existing works (e.g., [19, 17]).
•
Publicly Verifiable Validity: Validity of the data operations (e.g., whether the share/access is performed by an authorized user) can be publicly verified by anyone. This improves audit transparency such that all the data users do not need to participate during the audit process.
•
Temporal Access Control: Restricts the time for which the data can be accessed. This is useful when operating on highly sensitive data like EHRs.

We rigorously analyze the security of our proposed technique and prove that it achieves standard security notions (non-malleability, indistinguishability).
Finally, we implemented our technique and deployed it on a commodity platform (Amazon EC2) to evaluate its efficiency. Experimental results showed that 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} is highly scalable (§VII) and practical. Therefore, 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates}  can be used for sensitive data storage applications that require privacy-preserving audit log such as EHRs [13, 14] or supply chains [15, 16].

III Models
System Model.
In our system, there are three types of participants:
(i) The data owner 𝒪\mathcal{O} who owns some data;
(ii) The data user 𝒰\mathcal{U} who would like to access 𝒪\mathcal{O}’s data;
(iii) The storage provider 𝒮\mathcal{S} that provides storage facilities to store 𝒪\mathcal{O}’s data remotely.
Our system permits data storage, sharing, and access between these participants while, at the same time, recording all the data operations in a distributed audit log for auditability.
A Privacy-preserving and Immutable Audit Log (PIAL) scheme consists of the following PPT algorithms –
•
(ℒ,𝗉𝗉)←𝖨𝗇𝗂𝗍⁡(1λ,N)(\mathcal{L},\mathsf{pp})\leftarrow\mathsf{Init}(1^{\lambda},N): Given a security parameter λ\lambda, and the maximum number of supported operations NN, it outputs an initial distributed audit log ℒ\mathcal{L} and public parameters 𝗉𝗉\mathsf{pp}.
•
(𝗉𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋,𝗌𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋)←𝖱𝖾𝗀𝗂𝗌𝗍𝖾𝗋⁡(1λ)(\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}},\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}})\leftarrow\mathsf{Register}(1^{\lambda}): Given a security parameter λ\lambda, it outputs an address-key pair (𝗉𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋,𝗌𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋)(\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}},\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}}) as an identifier for the participant.
•
(𝗍𝗄𝗌𝗍𝗋,𝗍𝗑𝗌𝗍𝗋)←𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗋𝖾⁡(D,𝗌𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒪,𝗉𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒮,𝗉𝗉CLOSE(\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}},\mathsf{tx}_{\mathsf{str}})\leftarrow\mathsf{Store}(D,\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{O}},\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}},\mathsf{pp}): Given data DD, 𝒪\mathcal{O}’s private identity 𝗌𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒪\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{O}}, 𝒮\mathcal{S}’s public identity 𝗉𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒮\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}}, it outputs a store token 𝗍𝗄𝗌𝗍𝗋\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}} and a store record 𝗍𝗑𝗌𝗍𝗋\mathsf{tx}_{\mathsf{str}}.
•
(𝗍𝗄𝗈𝗐𝗇,𝗍𝗑𝗈𝗐𝗇)←𝖠𝗌𝗌𝗂𝗀𝗇𝖮𝗐𝗇𝖾𝗋⁡(𝗍𝗄𝗌𝗍𝗋,𝗌𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒮,𝗉𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒪,𝗉𝗉)(\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{own}},\mathsf{tx}_{\mathsf{own}})\leftarrow\mathsf{AssignOwner}(\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}},\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}},\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{O}},\mathsf{pp}):

Threat and Security Models.
We assume participants do not trust each other, i.e., they can behave maliciously toward the records created by other participants.
The adversary is curious about the record content, e.g., who is the owner of a particular data, whom the data is shared with or accessed by, and what data is being shared or accessed.
The adversary may also attempt to modify the record to compromise validity or to gain unauthorized access.
We aim to achieve integrity and confidentiality properties for the audit log via record non-malleability and audit log indistinguishability as follows.

IV Our Proposed Method
IV-A Overview
𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} supports three data operations – store, share, and access, wherein each operation is recorded in a distributed audit log (i.e., blockchain).
Fig. 1: Overview of 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} scheme.
The high-level workflow (ref. Figure 1)
is as follows, given a data dd to be stored on 𝒮\mathcal{S},
𝒪\mathcal{O} creates “store” record (1) and a token for 𝒮\mathcal{S} containing the message digest of dd, while sending dd off-chain to 𝒮\mathcal{S} for storage.
The nodes in the blockchain then verify the validity of the store record (2) before recording it to the audit log.
𝒮\mathcal{S} then creates an “ownership” record that certifies the ownership of 𝒪\mathcal{O} on the data and provides a token (3) that permits 𝒪\mathcal{O} to share her data with 𝒰\mathcal{U} later.
To share the data, 𝒪\mathcal{O} creates a “share” record (4) demonstrating her ownership, which is then verified by the blockchain before being appended to the audit log (5).
The user then accesses this record to obtain a share token 𝗍𝗄𝗌𝗁𝗋\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{shr}} (6) indicating the authorization to access the shared data stored on 𝒮\mathcal{S} for a limited period of time.
To access the shared data, the user creates an “access” record (7) demonstrating the access is authorized by the owner, and an access token for 𝒮\mathcal{S} to serve the request.
Finally,
the record (8) is verified by the blockchain and included in the audit log.
If it is valid, 𝒮\mathcal{S} sends data (9) to the requested user 𝒰\mathcal{U}.
To ensure all the data operations are confidential (e.g., hides participant identity or which data is being shared/accessed), while permitting public verification, we harness cryptographic commitment, encryption, and zero-knowledge techniques inspired by Zerocash [21].

IV-B3 Assign Ownership Protocol
𝒮\mathcal{S} performs an assign ownership operation on receiving a 𝗍𝗄𝗌𝗍𝗋\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}}.
𝒮\mathcal{S} decrypts 𝗍𝗄𝗌𝗍𝗋\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}} to identify which data needs to be assigned.
A serial number 𝗌𝗇\mathsf{sn} is generated using the seed ρ\rho (Figure 3, line 14) in the store record.
𝗌𝗇\mathsf{sn} uses a collision-resistant 𝖯𝖱𝖥\mathsf{PRF} function to ensure that only one assignment is created for the corresponding store operation.
Without this, 𝒮\mathcal{S} can use a single 𝗍𝗄𝗌𝗍𝗋\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{str}} to maliciously perform assign operations to multiple data owners.
Subsequently, 𝒮\mathcal{S} generates a commitment 𝖼𝗆𝗈𝗐𝗇←𝖢𝗈𝗆r′(𝗉𝗄𝖺𝖽𝖽𝗋𝒪||v′,𝗉𝗉)\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{own}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Com}_{r^{\prime}}(\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{addr}}^{\mathcal{O}}||v^{\prime},\mathsf{pp}) (Figure 3, line 15) to represent ownership assignment.
To successfully create an assign record, 𝒮\mathcal{S} generates a proof of ownership π𝗈𝗐𝗇\pi_{\mathsf{own}} (Figure 3, line 21).
Verification of the proof shows that 𝒮\mathcal{S} received a store request for the corresponding data.
The proof is generated by the following NP statements –
NP-statements for Data Owner Assignment.
Given an assign owner instance x=(𝗋𝗍𝗌𝗍𝗋,𝗌𝗇,𝖼𝗆𝗈𝗐𝗇,h)x=(\mathsf{rt}_{\mathsf{str}},\mathsf{sn},\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{own}},h), the witness w=(𝗉𝖺𝗍𝗁𝗌𝗍𝗋,𝗌𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒮,ρ,r,v,𝗉𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒮,r′,v′,𝗉𝗄𝖺𝖽𝗋𝒪,h𝗌𝗂𝗀)w=(\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}},\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}},\rho,r,v,\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{S}},r^{\prime},v^{\prime},\mathsf{pk}_{\mathsf{adr}}^{\mathcal{O}},h_{\mathsf{sig}}) is a valid witness for xx if the following holds –
•
Commitment 𝖼𝗆𝗌𝗍𝗋\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{str}} appears in the Merkle tree 𝗋𝗍𝗌𝗍𝗋\mathsf{rt}_{\mathsf{str}}, i.e., 𝗋𝗍𝗌𝗍𝗋=H(…(H(H(𝖼𝗆𝗌𝗍𝗋)||𝗉𝖺𝗍𝗁𝗌𝗍𝗋d)||𝗉𝖺𝗍𝗁𝗌𝗍𝗋d−1)||…||𝗉𝖺𝗍𝗁𝗌𝗍𝗋1))\mathsf{rt}_{\mathsf{str}}=H(\dots(H(H(\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{str}})||\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}}^{d})||\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}}^{d-1})||\dots||\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}}^{1})) where dd is the height of the Merkle tree and 𝗉𝖺𝗍𝗁𝗌𝗍𝗋i\mathsf{path}_{\mathsf{str}}^{i} represents the sibling node at height ii.
•
Commitments 𝖼𝗆𝗌𝗍𝗋\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{str}} and 𝖼𝗆𝗈𝗐𝗇\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{own}} are well-formed.
•

This is because we want to capture the state of the tree when the particular record was created.

To answer 𝖲𝗁𝖺𝗋𝖾\mathsf{Share} or 𝖠𝖼𝖼𝖾𝗌𝗌\mathsf{Access} queries, 𝒞\mathcal{C} computes hh, commitments (𝖼𝗆𝗌𝗁𝗋\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{shr}}/𝖼𝗆𝖺𝖼𝖼\mathsf{cm}_{\mathsf{acc}}), tokens (𝗍𝗄𝗌𝗁𝗋\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{shr}}/𝗍𝗄𝖺𝖼𝖼\mathsf{tk}_{\mathsf{acc}}), proofs (π𝗌𝗁𝗋\pi_{\mathsf{shr}}/π𝖺𝖼𝖼\pi_{\mathsf{acc}}) by following modifications in 𝖠𝗌𝗌𝗂𝗀𝗇𝖮𝗐𝗇𝖾𝗋\mathsf{AssignOwner}.
We prove this by constructing a sequence of hybrid games and show that they are indistinguishable as follows.
Let G0G_{0} be the original protocol IndGameΠ\texttt{IndGame}_{\Pi}. We define G1G_{1} as similar to G0G_{0}, except that 𝒞\mathcal{C} modifies the commitment in the response to use a 𝖼𝗆∗←𝖢𝗈𝗆r​(k,𝗉𝗉)\mathsf{cm}^{*}\leftarrow\mathsf{Com}_{r}(k,\mathsf{pp}) by using random inputs k,rk,r. We have that G1≈cG0G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}G_{0} due to the hiding property of the commitment scheme.We define G2G_{2} as similar to G1G_{1}, except that the token in the response is replaced with 𝗍𝗄∗←ℰ.𝖤𝗇𝖼⁡(e,p)\mathsf{tk}^{*}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}.\mathsf{Enc}(e,p) where ee is a random public key and pp is a random plaintext.
We argue that G2≈cG1G_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}G_{1} due to the IND-CCA and IK-CCA properties of the encryption scheme [24].
We define G3G_{3} as similar to G2G_{2}, except that all 𝖯𝖱𝖥\mathsf{PRF} generated values (i.e, h,𝗌𝗇h,\mathsf{sn}) in the response are replaced with values generated using a random function.
We say that G3≈cG2G_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}G_{2} due to the indistinguishability between 𝖯𝖱𝖥\mathsf{PRF} and random number function.
Finally, we define G4G_{4} as similar to G3G_{3}, except that the zero-knowledge proof in the response is replaced by a simulated proof π∗←S⁡(x,𝗉𝗉)\pi^{*}\leftarrow S(x,\mathsf{pp}).
We have that G4≈cG3G_{4}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}G_{3} due to the zero-knowledge property of the proof system.
We can see that G4G_{4} is the simulation IndGameS\texttt{IndGame}_{S}.
Therefore, IndGameΠ=G0≈cG1≈cG2≈cG3≈cG4=IndGameS\texttt{IndGame}_{\Pi}=G_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}G_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}G_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}G_{4}=\texttt{IndGame}_{S}.
Since IndGameS\texttt{IndGame}_{S} can simulate the response to QQ and Q′Q^{\prime} independent of bit bb, 𝒜\mathcal{A} can win IndGameΠ\texttt{IndGame}_{\Pi} game with negligible probability.

VI Implementation
We implemented 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} in Rust with 3,100 lines of code.
We used Hyperledger Fabric [25] to set up the audit log as the distributed ledger.
Each data operation was implemented as Hyperledger chaincode (equivalent to smart contracts) in Go.
For the signature scheme, we implemented Schnorr signature scheme [22].
For the asymmetric encryption, we implemented ECIES with AES-GCM as the underlying symmetric encryption scheme [24].
For the commitment scheme, we used Pedersen commitment [26].
We implemented the Merkle trees with Poseidon hash function as it can efficiently compute hashes of two elements at once [27].
We implemented MiMC as the primary hash function as well as PRF due to its low multiplicative complexity [23].
Finally, our implementation made use of Bulletproofs [20] as the back-end zero-knowledge proof due to its small proof size and transparent setup.
We prove/verify statements in zero-knowledge for each data operation as follows –
Store.
We implemented a circuit with one Pedersen commitment opening gadget 𝖯𝖾𝖽𝖦\mathsf{PedG} (based on [28]) to show knowledge of openings of a commitment and one hash pre-image gadget 𝖧𝗌𝗁𝖦\mathsf{HshG} (based on [23]) to show knowledge of a pre-image of a MiMC hash.
Assign.
We implemented a circuit with one 𝖬𝖾𝗆𝖦\mathsf{MemG}, two 𝖯𝖾𝖽𝖦\mathsf{PedG}, and two 𝖧𝗌𝗁𝖦\mathsf{HshG}. Here, 𝖬𝖾𝗆𝖦\mathsf{MemG} is a Merkle tree based proof of membership gadget.
Share.
We implemented a circuit with one range gadget 𝖱𝗀𝖾𝖦\mathsf{RgeG}, one 𝖧𝗌𝗁𝖦\mathsf{HshG}, one 𝖬𝖾𝗆𝖦\mathsf{MemG}, and two 𝖯𝖾𝖽𝖦\mathsf{PedG} gadgets. 𝖱𝗀𝖾𝖦\mathsf{RgeG} (based on [20]) is used to show knowledge that a value (𝗍𝗌\mathsf{ts} in our case) is within a given range.
Access.
We
implemented a circuit with 𝖱𝗀𝖾𝖦\mathsf{RgeG}, one 𝖧𝗌𝗁𝖦\mathsf{HshG}, one 𝖬𝖾𝗆𝖦\mathsf{MemG}, and two 𝖯𝖾𝖽𝖦\mathsf{PedG} gadgets.

VII-A Configuration
Hardware.
We used 50 AWS EC2 t3.medium instances to deploy a Hyperledger Fabric blockchain network.
Each instance had 4GB vRAM with a 2-core 3.1 GHz CPU (Intel Xeon) and network bandwidth of 4.3 Gbps.
Nodes were deployed in the us-east-1 region.
Parameter Setting.
We used standard parameters to achieve 128-bit security.
We used standard Poseidon Hash parameters for the Merkle tree including width t=6t=6, full rounds RF=8R_{F}=8 and partial rounds Rp=130R_{p}=130 [27].
We used MiMC hash function with rate 512512 and capacity 513513.
We implemented the Schnorr signature scheme to produce a 512-bit signature.
For the ECIES encryption scheme, our implementation used a 256-bit public and secret key.
We used Ed25519 curve of order 2255−192^{255}-19 for zero-knowledge proofs based on Bulletproofs, ECIES, and Schnorr. For Hyperledger Fabric, we set block size as 10 and the number of transaction confirmations as 1.

Circuit Complexity.
Recording store operations incur the least circuit complexity, compared with other operations such as assign, share, and access.
This is because performing a store operation only invokes one 𝖯𝖾𝖽𝖦\mathsf{PedG} and one 𝖧𝗌𝗁𝖦\mathsf{HshG}, while other operations invokes 𝖬𝖾𝗆𝖦\mathsf{MemG} along with other gadgets.
The constraint system of 𝖬𝖾𝗆𝖦\mathsf{MemG} is complicated and requires proving and verifying (in zero-knowledge) d=64d=64 Poseidon hash pre-images, each of which incurs 3×t×RF+3×Rp=5343\times t\times R_{F}+3\times R_{p}=534 number of multiplication constraints [27].
Assign operation incurs a little higher circuit complexity than share and access because of an extra 𝖧𝗌𝗁𝖦\mathsf{HshG} used for 𝗌𝗇\mathsf{sn}.
Share has one constraint over access as it requires an additional constraint to calculate difference between the current timestamp, 𝖼𝗎𝗋𝗋\mathsf{curr} and the share expiry timestamp, 𝗍𝗌\mathsf{ts} (see §IV-B5).
Processing Latency.
The processing latency depends on circuit complexity, especially the number of multiplication gates (|M||M|).
Store operation incurs a small latency compared with other operations because it does not require checking set membership, which attributes to large circuit complexity.
We include the Common Reference String (CRS) loading delay that is needed for the backend zero-knowledge proof (i.e., Bulletproofs), which takes around 1.51.5 seconds (s) in total.
Without CRS, the proving and verification time for store operation is 0.35 s and 85 milliseconds (ms), respectively, which are about 60 times lesser than assign, share, and access.
This matches their difference in |M|\lvert M\rvert.
Memory Usage.
The memory usage also depends on the circuit complexity to prove each data operation.
The CRS component takes approximately 3232 MB.
Proving incurs more memory usage than verification, due to the space needed to store the witness.
Bandwidth.
Since Bulletproof incurs a polylogarithmic proof size, the bandwidth overhead for each data operation is small.
The proof size produced by Bulletproofs is 2​⌈log2⁡(|M|)⌉+132\lceil\log_{2}(|M|)\rceil+13 group elements.
For store, |M|=644|M|=644 so its proof contains 2​⌈log2⁡(644)⌉+13=332\lceil\log_{2}(644)\rceil+13=33 group elements, which results in a total of 32⋅33=1,05632\cdot 33=1,056 bytes (B).
For assign, share, and access operations, |M|≈37,000|M|\approx 37,000 and, their proof size is 1,440 B.

VIII Related Work
Centralized Audit Log.
Traditional data sharing/access schemes record data operations directly on the server that stores the data [29, 30].
Such records are needed for audit purposes, where a third-party auditor determines whether any unauthorized accesses have been made in the system [1, 31].
However, with the introduction of regulations like HIPAA a secure logging mechanism [10] is needed.
Secure logging primitives [11, 32] rely on a trusted author whose signature is considered the root of trust for integrity or requires analyzing the entirety of the audit log to detect tampering due to the privacy-preserving nature of the records.
Alternatively, prior works [33, 12] use secure hardware to achieve immutable and privacy-preserving records.
Since the audit log is maintained by the centralized server,
it is vulnerable to a single point of failure,
where the corrupted server can compromise
the confidentiality and integrity of the audit log.
Decentralized Audit Log.
Several works have proposed a decentralized audit log to address the single point of failure vulnerabilities.
These schemes were designed for medical data [3, 13], supply chain [15, 16], and financial applications [34, 35].
Some schemes permit tamper resistance and access control to data objects being logged [36, 37].
However, these approaches do not hide the user identities (e.g., sender/receiver).
Several works attempted to achieve either full anonymity [18] or partially anonymous [19].
There are several schemes that permit the validity of audit log to be verified publicly [17] or privately [18, 19].
Private validity verifiability requires every user in the system to participate and prove the integrity of their records during the audit phase.
IX Conclusion
We proposed 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates}, a privacy-preserving and immutable audit log scheme.
𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} achieved immutability and resistance to single point of failure by leveraging blockchain, and achieved public verifiability of record validity using zero-knowledge proofs.
We fully implemented 𝖧𝖺𝗋𝗉𝗈𝖼𝗋𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗌\mathsf{Harpocrates} and evaluated its performance by using Hyperledger Fabric and Amazon EC2.
