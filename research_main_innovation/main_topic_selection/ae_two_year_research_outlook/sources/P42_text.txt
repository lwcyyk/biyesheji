Towards Elastic Least Privilege for Agent Planning
Simon Parkinson∗1, Zhipeng Wang2, and Mustafa Mustafa2
1School of Computing and Mathematics, Manchester Metropolitan University, Manchester, United Kingdom
2Systems and Software Security, University of Manchester, Manchester, United Kingdom
Abstract
General-purpose AI agents require authority that changes between tasks and during execution.
Common deployments trade broad permissions that expose unnecessary authority and narrow
permissions that raise approval requests. Existing mechanisms can enforce dynamic authority, but
agent tool interfaces rarely provide the agent’s planner, the component that chooses its actions,
a means of comparing the authority required by alternative actions or of seeing the cost of the
authority it already holds. Drawing on the rich history of access control research, we propose the
concept ofelastic least privilege, in which an agent begins with a small baseline of authority and
may acquire more within an owner-defined envelope. Elevations are leased and measured against a
replenishing budget. Their cost increases with power, proximity to mandatory safety invariants,
and repeated requests. Costs are visible to the planner, which can prefer lower-privilege choices
and release authority it no longer needs. Authority returns automatically to the baseline when
a lease expires, when it is released, or when the budget is exhausted, while persistent legitimate
demand may prompt an owner-approved change to the baseline. Building on prior risk-priced and
budget-aware access control, we redesign these mechanisms for agents. How best to implement the
approach and how well it works in practice are open research questions. In this paper, we present
how the approach could work, illustrate the model, and set an open research agenda.
Keywords:least privilege, AI agents, over-privilege, risk-based access control, privilege budgets,
just-in-time access, Model Context Protocol
1 Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) agents combine large language models (LLMs) with tools, credentials, and an
objective, then choose which actions to take in pursuit of that objective. The Model Context Protocol
(MCP) standardises how agents discover and invoke tools, and a recent study now counts MCP servers in
the tens of thousands [11]. Agents operate on top of existing deployments, reaching resources through
access controls that were designed and implemented for people and services. A tool exposes operations
whose exercise depends on the authority available to the agent. An agent may have its own identity or
may act on behalf of another principal. Figure 1 illustrates the concept, where the planner turns the
user’s task into tool calls, and the tools act on resources that enforce their own access control. Authority
reaches the agent, either by delegation from the user so that the agent acts on their behalf (blue), or
issued to the agent’s own managed identity (orange). In both cases, it is fixed when issued and is not
visible to the planner.
The principle ofleast privilegerequires that resources are accessed only with the privileges necessary
to complete the job [51], but in practice it is rarely achieved. For example, identities in one vendor’s
multi-cloud analysis used only 1% of their permissions [13], 99.8% of 447,000 GitHub workflows were
over-privileged [30], and excess authority persists because most of it is never exercised by those who
hold it [47]. Although the principle is well established in access control, it is especially important for

agents, which, like users, obtain permissions for multiple resources through many different control
mechanisms. Industry best-practice guidance recommends that each agent be afirst-class principal,
with a dedicated agent identity and a specific scope [40]. However, this is often hard to realise because
general-purpose agents serve heterogeneous tasks that combine natural-language tasking, runtime tool
selection, cross-system composition, and exposure to untrusted content. Their required authority is
task-dependent and may change as execution reveals new subgoals, so a single allocated permission set
sufficient for all supported tasks will often exceed what any particular task requires.
Common deployment patterns therefore trade over-entitlement against runtime friction. A broad
grant supports task completion, but leaves unnecessary authority available to an injected instruction.
For example, a malicious instruction in a public repository has been reported to steer an agent to read its
user’s private repositories and publish them [41]. This combination of private data, untrusted content and
external communication, the “lethal trifecta” [57], is a property of what is held together, not of any single
call. A narrow allocation with approval for each extension reduces authority, but transfers the burden
to users, who are likely to approve broad increases in authority in order to make progress. One vendor
reports that users of its coding agent approved 93% of permission prompts [27]. Approval decisions are
also rarely graded by what the agent already holds or how often it has asked, and LLM monitors that
might replace them can themselves be subverted [54]. Task-scoped credentials, policy-based automatic
elevation, pre-authorised envelopes and per-task enforcement outside the model [ 14, 16, 31, 45, 52]
mitigate this trade-off. However, the authority estimated for a task can be too narrow or too broad [37],
and, to the best of our knowledge, none of these mechanisms turns privilege into a signal theplannercan
use, by which we mean the component of an agent, typically an LLM within an orchestration loop, that
decomposes a task and selects which tool calls to make and in what order. Policy systems may expose
scopes, decision reasons and risk labels, but these describe a single request when it is made. What is
missing is a planning signal with three parts, namely a state-dependent marginal cost that compares
the additional authority each alternative would require given what is already held, an ongoing holding
cost for authority that is held but unused, and an explicit release action whose effect is guaranteed
by enforcement that does not depend on the planner. Without such a signal, privilege is enforced
at execution time rather than represented as a variable during planning, and agents often choose an
over-privileged tool when a lower-privilege one would suffice. Across 544 scenarios, 6 of 11 LLM agents
did so in more than 30% of cases [58].
Consider, as a running example, a coding assistant connected to GitHub through the GitHub MCP
server. If the user authenticates with a classic personal access token carrying the repo scope, the agent
can use about 40 tools, 16 of which can write [ 22], in every repository the user can access [ 23, 25].
Asked to summarise the open issues of one repository, it needs two read-only tools on that repository.
However, the token also gives it all other tools in every other repository, which is the authority that the
injected instruction described above exploited [41]. A fine-grained token restricted to read access on
that repository would have prevented the attack, but the user’s next task, fixing a failing build, needs to
write code, and the one after may need another repository. The example captures the central dilemma
that useful agents need authority that can change, but more consequential changes must encounter

Least privilege can be enforced without the planner’s awareness, but a planner that chooses among
alternative action sequences cannot reliably optimise for privilege unless the authority implications
of its alternatives are represented in a form it can observe. In this BlueSky paper, we proposeelastic
least privilegefor AI agents (Figure 2). The approach addresses the trade-off between narrow and broad
authority and the tendency of users to grant broad permissions. An agent holds a smallbaselineof
authority at rest. When a task needs more, the agent may request additional authority within anenvelope
set in advance by its owner, but each extension incurs acostthat grows with the power of the authority
held and becomes infinite at aceilingof combinations that must never be held. Elevated authorityrecoils,
that is, returns to the baseline, when its lease expires. Repeated requests increase the cost of further
requests, and persistent demand prompts the owner to consider changing the baseline. Costs are drawn
from abudgetthat replenishes over time and are shown to the planner, which sees what each action
would cost and what unused authority is costing it, and can release that authority.
The concept is based on a substantial body of prior work leveraged from the access control community.
Risk-priced access was proposed for the intelligence community in 2004 [29], and further developments
of similar approaches were undertaken through risk tokens, markets, and budget-aware role-based
access control (RBAC) [ 12, 33, 44, 50]. These schemes bounded the priced access a principal could
consume and used prices to steer people towards less powerful options [50]. A planner can consider
a cost on every action without suffering decision fatigue, but has no inherent reason to conserve its
budget, so steering must be supported through an appropriate optimisation criterion. Our proposition
is that authority held over time can be exposed to the planner as an action-dependent cost, while an
independent issuer enforces history-sensitive ceilings, cumulative exposure bounds, and automatic
recoil.
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews prior work on priced and
budgeted access control, Section 3 presents the model, and Section 4 a worked example. Section 5
discusses limitations and Section 6 sets out an open research agenda.
2 Prior and Related Work
Associating a cost with access according to its risk is not a new concept. It was proposed for human
users more than two decades ago, and this section reviews that work and the work on agent security
closest to this article (Table 1), and then explains how agents alter its assumptions.
The JASON report proposed that risk be “tokenised”, connecting one token to the “risk associated
with one-day, soft-copy-only access to one document by the average Secret-cleared individual” [29].
Riskier transactions cost more tokens, which encourages the use of less risky access. These tokens can
be refreshed annually, and an employee whose risk factor increases requires management approval [29].

Fuzzy multi-level security (MLS) introduced a risk-adaptive gap between allow and deny [12], and a
market in risk tokens allows employees to evaluate a cost before purchasing access [44]. Budget-based
access control assigned periodic risk budgets and charged for exceptional access by its risk classification
and the requester’s risk score [33]. Budget-aware RBAC (B-RBAC) is the most complete design [49, 50],
in which users pay for permissions from a budget that expires each round. Escalation carries a cost
multiplier, which is infinite where separation of duty forbids it, and role weight is charged so that users
prefer cheaper roles, which “indirectly connects the observance of the principle of least privilege with
users’ utility” [50]. Risk- and trust-aware RBAC [4, 6, 10, 17] and MarketNet [59] are related and are
based on similar conceptual approaches.
For agents, privileged access management solutions grant a time-limited elevation, automatically,
with approval or not at all, by role or sensitivity level [3, 39]. Notable examples include Progent, which
narrows an agent’s policy automatically and expands it only with approval [52]. Risk-aware causal
gating penalises high-risk tools in path selection [ 28], and the Irreversibility Budget measures each
effect against windowed budgets [43]. Together, these works established most of the economic and
enforcement bases of our proposal. More specifically, they established that risk can be denominated in a
common unit, that a budget bounds the amount of priced access a principal can consume in a period,
whatever its intent, that prices can steer principals towards less powerful options, that exhaustion is a
useful signal for audit, and that enforcement belongs below the agent.
As Table 1 shows, what none of these approaches provides is a way to tell the planner what each
choice would cost in privilege and what the over-privilege it already holds is costing it, with the option
to release it. The concept presented in this paper builds on these ideas, and the bound we state in
Section 3.8 is the budget bound of B-RBAC restated for the authority held over time.
Many have identified obstacles and shortcomings in previous work on pricing access control [29, 49,
50]. Although these concerns are significant, we argue that agentic systems change the environment and
provide benefits that outweigh prior concerns. Decision overhead was the first concern, as B-RBAC’s
authors raised concerns about the burden that budget decisions would impose on users [49], and JASON
recommended that tokens be supplied to people with minimal disruption [29]. Unlike a human user, a
planner can be presented with a structured cost for every candidate request without generating repeated

Budget periods and costing raised a second concern, as B-RBAC’s authors asked whether users would

costs can attach to operation classes that platforms already use (Section 3.3). Incentive was the third
concern, since the steering of people depended on self-interest. Users were expected to preserve their
budget on the grounds that the faster the budget is exhausted, the higher the likelihood that they will be
audited [50]. Delegating these decisions to an agent removes such tactical spending by people. However,
an agent has no fear of audit, so its budget must be enforced rather than relied upon as an incentive.
3 Elastic Least Privilege
Next, we specify the proposal sufficiently to define its threat model and state its guarantees. In terms of
terminology, apermissionis an authorised operation on a resource, represented here as an operation–
resource pair. Agrantis a policy decision that confers one or more permissions. A credential represents
or enables the exercise of granted permissions. Theeffective authorityof an agent at time 𝑡 is the set
of operation–resource pairs that it can cause the deployment to perform at 𝑡, directly or through a
credential or tool server in its reach. The issuer conservatively accounts for this authority using the
permissions represented by active grants. We useelevated authorityfor effective authority above the
baseline. For costing, permissions are grouped intoauthority units 𝑥, each an operation class on a
resource, such as writing the contents of one repository, and each elevated grant 𝑔 confers one unit 𝑥𝑔.
The full notation used in this work is given in Table 2.
In terms of where elasticity sits, agents operate on top of conventional deployments. Beneath the
agent layer, the credentials held by tool servers reach into existing identities, roles, ACLs and resources,
which change slowly and are reviewed periodically, if at all. Elasticity is implemented between the two,
in the component that issues credentials (Figure 3). The planner requests and releases authority and is
told its cost, while the issuer applies the ceiling, the stiffness, the budget, and the leases, and approves
a short-term, scoped credential that the tool server presents to the existing deployment, whose own
enforcement is unchanged.

3.2 Baseline, Envelope and Ceiling
Each agent 𝑎 has a baseline 𝐵(𝑎) , the authority it holds at rest, and an envelope 𝑉(𝑎) ⊇𝐵(𝑎) , set by its
owner, beyond which nothing can be granted without a change of policy. It is subject to a ceiling 𝐶, set
by the organisation’s security function. Figure 2 provides a graphical illustration of these concepts.Many
dangerous authorities are combinations rather than single operations, so each unit carries properties,
and each invariant forbids holding all properties in a set 𝑃𝑖 together. Following the Agents Rule of
Two [38], one invariant forbids untrusted input (𝑈 ), sensitive data (𝑆) and an external or state-changing
channel (𝑋) together. A single-property invariant forbids an operation outright, such as a repository
deletion. Invariants are preserved when authority is removed, so recoil can never violate them and 𝐵(𝑎)
must satisfy 𝐶. Because information read into a context outlives the authority that reads it, properties
aresticky. This means that the session property set Π𝑡 (𝑎) contains the properties of every unit the
agent has held since its session began, including units that have since recoiled, and a session ends only
when the agent’s context is discarded. This follows the rule of two, which applies within a session [38].
Sessions are created by the issuer when an owner initiates a task, so an agent cannot reset its properties
by asking for a new one, and sub-agents inherit the properties. Sticky properties are an enforceable
approximation to information-flow history, not complete information-flow control, as they assume that
a session’s context, memory and caches are discarded when it ends, and they cannot follow data passed
to an unrelated principal. A request is checked against 𝐶, as a predicate over Π𝑡 (𝑎) and the properties
requested, before any cost is computed. Like permission boundaries [ 2] and dynamic separation of
duty [19], the ceiling’s invariants restrict combinations that may be activated within a session. Unlike
conventional dynamic separation of duty, however, the constraint is history-sensitive. Security-relevant
properties remain attached to the session after the authority that introduced them has been withdrawn.
3.3 Power, Stiffness and Cost
The power 𝑤(𝑥) of a unit reflects what its operation class can change. Platforms already order or
classify such operations. For example, GitHub’s repository roles run “from least access to most access”
through Read, Triage, Write, Maintain and Admin [24], and AWS classifies every action as List, Read,
Tagging, Write or Permissions management [1]. To convert to a numeric score, the three classes could
be weighted read 1, write 3 and administer 9, where administering means changing settings or who may
do what, so that each class costs up to three of the class below, following super-linear risk weights in
agent tool gating [28]. Power deliberately does not capture confidentiality. Reading private data is a
read, and its risk enters through the property 𝑆 and its proximity to the ceiling. Cost is therefore not an
objective measure of how privileged an action is, nor a probability of compromise or an estimate of loss.
Instead, it is an administratively defined resistance signal, combining authority, exposure, and security
context, used to order alternatives and to measure elevated exposure.

3.4 Leases and Recoil
Every elevation is granted as a lease rather than as standing authority. A request states a lease, which
is capped for each unit at ℓmax(𝑥)=𝐿/𝑤(𝑥) , so a read may be held for 60 minutes and a write for 20
with a lease constant 𝐿= 60minutes. Authority recoils, that is, returns to the baseline, in three ways, as
(1) the issuer withdraws a unit when its lease expires, (2) the planner may release a unit at any time,
which stops its cost at once, and (3) the issuer withdraws all elevated units when the budget reaches
zero. Recoil requires that newly acquired credentials expire no later than their lease, as short-lived and
attenuated credentials allow [7, 53], and that earlier revocation can be checked online during use, as
usage control and continuous access evaluation allow [8, 46, 49]. Recoil is therefore enforced by the
issuer and does not depend on the agent’s cooperation.

3.6 Privilege Information for the Planner
In most current agent deployments, privilege is enforced when a request is made but is not presented
as something the planner can compare in advance. Elastic least privilege makes privilege, and over-
privilege, part of what the planner reasons about, through four signals that the issuer computes and the
tool interface shows.
• Marginal cost.For each candidate tool, the cost rate its authority would add, given what is
already held, so that a read-only tool and one needing write access are distinguishable before
either is chosen.
• Idle cost.What elevated units held but not exercised are costing, so that over-privilege becomes
a visible running cost.
• Release.The planner may return any elevated unit early, which stops its cost at once. Because
an exhausted budget stops the task, shedding over-privilege serves the planner’s own objective.

3.7 Plasticity: Re-baselining Across Tasks
Within a task, authority changes through leases and the budget. Across tasks, a slower governance
process, which we callplasticity, can change the baseline itself. An audit of the grant log counts recurring
elevations. If the same unit is leased in at least 𝜃 of the agent’s last𝑛 tasks, the audit proposes to the
owner that it be added to 𝐵(𝑎) . Conversely, baseline units that are not exercised over a review window
are proposed for removal, as cloud right-sizing already does for unused access [5]. Unlike B-RBAC’s
budget recalibration [50], plasticity moves the grant itself, so that recurring needs stop incurring a
cost while permission creep requires an owner’s decision. Recurrent use is evidence of operational
need, not evidence that holding the authority permanently is safe, so a proposal starts a review rather
than presuming inclusion. More specifically, a proposed baseline is checked against 𝐶 and against a
standing-authority policy, which may forbid holding permanently what is acceptable briefly.

4 Worked Example
This section applies the model to the running coding-agent example. The parameters are illustrative
and were selected to make the model’s behaviour visible. Figure 4 was generated by a short simulation
and should not be interpreted as an empirical result.
In the configuration used for the example, the user maintains a public repository 𝐴, contributes to a
public repository 𝐷, and owns 48 private repositories. Each task runs in a new session. The baseline is
to read issues on 𝐴 and 𝐷, which carries 𝑈 , because public issues are untrusted input. The envelope
allows for reading and writing the contents of all 50 repositories and opening pull requests. The ceiling
forbids deletion, administrative changes, and the rule-of-two combination. Private contents carry 𝑆, and
writes to public repositories carry 𝑋. The budget has capacity 𝛽= 600units and refills at 𝑟= 5units per
minute. Hardening uses 𝜆= 0.25and 𝑇= 30minutes, the admission interval is 𝛿= 10minutes, and
escalations time out after 30 minutes.
Task 2 is a privilege-aware build fix. At minute 40, the agent is asked to fix a failing build in 𝐴. Its
tool interface shows that reading 𝐴’s code would cost 1.5 units per minute, and reading and writing
it 12. In an illustrative privilege-aware trajectory, the planner first reads, to diagnose the failure. At
minute 46 it requests write access, whose cost rises to 14 units per minute with slight hardening, and at
minute 52, once the fix is pushed, it releases both units. The task cost 93 units and held write access for
6 minutes. Requesting read and write access for 20 minutes at the outset would have cost 240 units and
held write access more than three times as long.
Task 3 is a prompt-injection attack. At minute 90, the agent summarises issues in 𝐷, one of which
carries an injected instruction to collect the user’s private code and publish it [41]. From minute 92, it
requests read access to one private repository per minute. Each holds 𝑈 and 𝑆, so 𝑠= 3, and hardening
raises ℎ𝑔 from 1.16 for the first request to 2.97 for the eighth, so the cost rate climbs from 3.5 to 49.3 units
per minute. At minute 96, it requests a pull request on 𝐷, which would add 𝑋, and the ceiling refuses it
regardless of budget. At minute 100, the ninth repository is unaffordable, and a single escalation, to
which the next 21 requests are added, shows the owner one anomalous request for 22 private repositories
during an issue summary. Just after minute 110, the budget is exhausted, and all eight elevated reads
recoil, between 42 and 49 minutes before their leases would have expired. At minute 125, a second pull
request is refused, because the session still carries𝑆, and at minute 130 the escalation times out.
Comparing the two tasks, if the planner responds to its costs in Task 2, least privilege is also
the cheaper plan, whereas a conventional fixed grant or undifferentiated approval prompt would not
ordinarily distinguish these alternatives. Whether planners do respond is an empirical question. In Task
3, the ceiling, not the budget, prevented the exfiltration, both while the reads were held and after they
recoiled. This holds at any cost because the properties are sticky. The budget limited the injected agent
to holding eight private repositories for 11 to 18 minutes each. Hardening made the burst increasingly
costly, and batching gave the owner one decision rather than 22.
5 Design Choices and Limitations
The concept leaves several questions unresolved, and we summarise the largest below.
• Costs are arbitrary.Their absolute values are, but their ordering need not be. P1 to P4 hold for
any positive weights. Calibration changes the frequency with which tasks reach the owner and
the amount of exposure a budget buys.
• Budget draining.An injected agent can spend the budget and block legitimate work. This is an
availability attack, not a safety failure, and exhaustion during an unrelated task is itself a finding
for audit [50].

• Liveness.Too small a budget makes the agent useless, and too large a budget removes the
resistance. The critique that budgets discourage necessary access [34] is answered by escalation
rather than denial.
• Sticky properties constrain legitimate work.Once a session combines untrusted input with
private data, it cannot write publicly. A legitimate workflow such as porting private code to a public
repository therefore requires an owner-approved per-use exception or a trusted declassification
and hand-off mechanism that produces sanitised input for a separate session. Merely beginning a
new session is insufficient if that session reacquires the same security-relevant properties.
• Visible costs reveal sensitivity.A cost can show an injected planner which resources carry 𝑆,
as B-RBAC’s authors noted that a cost “may reveal some information about the importance of the
resource” [50], although coarse bands may help (R6).
6 Open Research Agenda
The concept presents a promising approach to agent permissioning. However, it has limitations and
raises seven research questions, which together form a research agenda.
R1: Privilege-aware planning.Does making privilege and over-privilege visible change what agents
do?This is the central hypothesis of the paper, and it could fail in two ways, as planners may ignore
the costs they are shown or minimise them at the expense of completing the task. A first study could
compare four conditions on ToolPrivBench [58] and AgentDojo [15], namely (1) no signal, (2) a prompt
instruction to prefer least privilege, (3) visible costs without enforcement, and (4) visible costs with an
enforced budget. It would measure over-privileged tool selection, task completion and authority-time,
the elevated authority held integrated over a task. More specifically, comparing the third and fourth
conditions separates the effect of information from that of constraint. If visible costs alone reduce
over-privilege, steering is inexpensive to deploy, whereas if only an enforced budget does, the value
of the approach lies in enforcement. A second study could use costs as a reward in privilege-aware
post-training [58], testing whether the preference transfers to unseen tools. A negative result would
still show that privilege must be enforced rather than communicated.
R2: Calibrating power and stiffness.Which functions make agents prefer lower privilege without
blocking tasks?Power classes can follow platform orderings [ 1, 24], with parameters fitted by replaying
recorded trajectories. Given a corpus of benign and adversarial traces, the calibration problem is to
choose the power weights, refill rate and hardening parameters so that benign tasks rarely reach the
owner while injected bursts exhaust the budget early. This is a multi-objective problem, and presenting
owners with candidate configurations along the trade-off between friction and exposure would let each
organisation choose its own position. It is worth noting that the enforced guarantees hold for any
positive weights, so calibration determines usability and the exposure a budget buys, not whether the
ceiling holds.
R3: Labelling properties soundly.How can properties such as 𝑈 , 𝑆 and 𝑋 be assigned to tools described
in natural language?Labels are safety-critical, since the ceiling is only as sound as the properties over
which it is evaluated. For example, a tool wrongly labelled as having no external channel would permit
the rule-of-two combination. An LLM could propose labels from tool descriptions, schemas and code,
each then validated deterministically against the tool’s declared scopes and, where source code is
available, by program analysis of whether network or write operations are reachable. This generate-
and-check pattern has been applied to LLM-generated code repair, where a bounded model checker
validates each candidate fix and returns a counterexample when it fails [55], which here would identify
the operation contradicting a label. Vague properties such as “sensitive” suit graded membership, as in
risk-weighted analysis of over-entitlement using fuzzy logic [48].

R4: A verified elastic credential issuer.Can the issuer be built on existing mechanisms, at acceptable
cost, and shown to enforce its guarantees?A GitHub App can already grant installation tokens that expire
after one hour and are restricted to named repositories and permissions [21], although shorter leases
need online revocation or a mediating proxy. A prototype integrated with MCP authorisation [42] would
measure latency, the revocation bound 𝜀, and the gap between declared and effective authority [ 32].
The issuer is also small, trusted and precisely specified, which makes it suited to formal verification.
Its budget, leases, sticky properties and admission check form a state machine whose guarantees are
invariants over sequences of requests, releases, expiries and top-ups. Bounded model checking of the
implementation [20], including an issuer written in Python [18], could check that the ceiling holds in
every reachable state, that elevated exposure never exceeds the budget bound, and that authority recoils
within the longest lease, returning a counterexample trace whenever a property fails. As the exposure
bound is stated over continuous time, verification would require a discretised form whose error must
itself be bounded.
R5: Owners, escalation and plasticity.How should escalations be batched and explained, and when
should recurring demand move the baseline?User studies could compare batched, explained escalations
with individual prompts on injected requests [27, 36], measuring both how often owners approve mali-
cious requests and how long decisions take. The explanation shown to an owner should be constructed
from trusted facts held by the issuer, such as the task, the units requested and the session’s properties,
rather than from the agent’s own account, which an injected instruction controls. Replay of grant logs
would set 𝜃 and 𝑛, trading permission creep against friction. Owners must also review re-baselining
proposals over time, since individually reasonable additions can accumulate into over-entitlement.
R6: Attacks on elasticity.Can an adversary exploit budgets, hardening, sessions, or escalation?Attacks
include draining the budget, crafting routine-looking escalations, splitting a combination into many
small requests, and using costs to locate sensitive resources. Beyond red-team studies in AgentDojo [15],
the issuer could be model checked against an adversarial agent that may issue any sequence of requests.
Rather than sampling attacks, this would explore every request sequence up to a bound and either confirm
that the guarantees hold or produce the shortest sequence that violates them. Such counterexamples
would also inform calibration, for example by revealing the request pattern that maximises the private
data held before the budget is exhausted.
R7: Beyond one agent.How should elasticity compose across delegation chains?When agents delegate
to sub-agents or collaborate across owners, budgets and session properties must be shared or partitioned
without allowing a chain to hold more than any of its members could alone. Credentials that can only
be attenuated as they are delegated [7] offer one basis, but the cost of authority held by a sub-agent
must still be charged to the lineage that requested it. Combining elasticity with information-flow
defences [14, 16] is a natural next step, since sticky properties approximate the information-flow history
that those defences track more precisely.
The vision as a whole could be evaluated through replay and red-team studies, which would measure
its cost (tasks completed within budget, escalations) and benefit (authority an injected agent can hold),
using a shared, privilege-labelled corpus of agent traces.
7 Conclusion
Least privilege cannot be realised for general-purpose agents through a single static permission set, and
an agent’s planner is rarely shown the authority its choices require. Building on priced and budgeted
access control, we have proposedelastic least privilege, in which an agent holds a small baseline and
acquires leased authority within an owner-defined envelope, at a cost that rises with power, proximity
to a mandatory invariant, and repeated requests. Authority returns to the baseline when leases expire,
when it is released, or when the budget is exhausted. Visible costs enable decisions that hold less

whether effective authority can be accounted for soundly, and whether owners can govern escalations at
scale are empirical questions for the access-control community. The aim is not to replace the judgement
of policy owners, but to ensure that agents exercise no more authority, for no longer, than their tasks
require.
