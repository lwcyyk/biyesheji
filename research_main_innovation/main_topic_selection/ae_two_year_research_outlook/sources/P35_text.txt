 a b s t r a c t
Manual generation of access control policies from high-level organizational requirements is labor-intensive and 
error-prone, often leading to critical failures and data breaches. While automated frameworks have been pro-
posed, existing approaches struggle with complex access requirements due to poor domain adaptation, limiting 
their accuracy. To address these challenges, we propose AGentVLM, a novel access control policy generation 
and veriﬁcation framework based on small, open-source language models (LMs). Our framework enables its eﬃ-
cient on-premise deployment, preserving data conﬁdentiality by avoiding reliance on third-party black-box LMs. 
AGentVLM excels in identifying natural language access control policies (NLACPs) from high-level requirements, 
achieving an average F1 score of 90.6 %. Unlike existing frameworks limited to generating simple policies with 
three components (subject, action, resource), AGentVLM eﬀectively extracts complex elements such as purposes 
and conditions using an access control-speciﬁc structured information extraction technique. This method cap-
tures both word-level and semantic information at the same time from NLACPs, leading to a state-of-the-art 
policy generation F1 score of 80.6 %. Additionally, AGentVLM introduces a veriﬁcation technique that provides 
actionable feedback, allowing administrators to reﬁne inaccurate policies before deployment. To support future 
research, we also release two annotated datasets addressing the scarcity of domain-speciﬁc data.
1.  Introduction
In June 2023, Microsoft AI researchers released a GitHub repository, 
oﬀering users access to open-source code and AI models for an image 
recognition task via an Azure storage URL (Uniform Resource Locator) 
[1]. However, instead of allowing users only to read the source code and 
AI models, the storage account administrator (a researcher or a system 
administrator) has inadvertently given “full access” to the entire storage 
account [1]. As a result, 38 terabytes of sensitive information, includ-
ing passwords to Microsoft services stored in the storage account, were 
leaked to the general public [1].
One of the main reasons for such incidents is the mistakes made by 
system administrators when manually generating/conﬁguring the ini-
tial set of access control policies based on the organization’s high-level 
requirement speciﬁcations [2–4]. These speciﬁcations are written by 
security experts in natural language (NL) outlining how the organiza-
tion’s information should be accessed, by whom, and under what cir-
cumstances [5]. Therefore, we call them natural language access control 
policies (NLACPs) [6]. However, these NLACPs are often unstructured,

To help automate this task, previous research introduced fully auto-

like subject, action, and resource) [7,9,11,15,16]. To this end, they use 
machine learning (ML) and natural language processing (NLP) tech-
niques without involving system administrators, attempting to reduce 
access control failures due to human mistakes [2,8].
However, the existing approaches are far from being reliable due 
to several limitations [2,6,8,9,17]. Speciﬁcally, they cannot be used to 
generate enforceable policies from NLACPs that contain policy compo-
nents with complex linguistic structures like purposes and conditions

poses and conditions that need to be checked before allowing an access 
request [8]. Furthermore, existing deep learning-based access control 
policy generation frameworks are not adapted to the access control do-
main [7,9,14,19]. Instead, they depend on general-purpose deep learn-
ing models [6,7,9] like Semantic Role Labeling (SRL) models, which 
are not speciﬁcally designed for extracting access control policy com-

policy components but also miss required policy components, leading 
to low precision and recall, respectively [7,9]. These limitations of ex-
isting frameworks make them unreliable and, in turn, unsuitable for an 
automated, accurate access control policy generation [7,9]. Therefore, 
this paper proposes a novel access control policy generation and veriﬁ-
cation framework to improve the accuracy of the access control policy 
generation process, making the following key contributions.
• We introduce AGentVLM, a novel access control policy generation 
and veriﬁcation framework based on a unique adaptation of lan-
guage models (LMs) to identify and translate the organization’s ac-
cess control requirements. Since it utilizes “small” open-source LMs, 
it can be eﬃciently  implemented within the organization to generate 
policies, preserving the conﬁdentiality of the organization’s access 
control requirements [20] (Section 3).
• We introduce a novel access control-speciﬁc structured information 
extraction technique that not only extracts token/word-level infor-
mation from NLACPs (e.g., policy components) but also information 
hidden in NLACPs’ meaning (e.g., access control rule (ACR) deci-
sions) at the same time, to generate granular access control policies 
(Section 3).
• We introduce a novel LM-based access control policy veriﬁcation 
technique that not only identiﬁes speciﬁc errors in the generated ac-
cess control policy but also locates them within the policy through 
explainable artiﬁcial intelligence (XAI) [21]. This helps administra-
tors ﬁnd and reﬁne errors in the incorrectly generated access control 
policies before adding them to the authorization system (Section 3).
• We evaluate AGentVLM using real-world high-level requirement 
speciﬁcations, showing that it identiﬁes NLACPs with a state-of-the-
art F1 score of 90.6 % (Section 5.2.1). We further demonstrate its 
ability to translate the identiﬁed NLACPs into granular access con-
trol policies with ﬁve essential components (i.e., subject, action, re-
source, purpose, and condition), achieving a mean state-of-the-art 
F1 score of 80.6 % (Section 5.2.2). Finally, we show that AGentVLM 
identiﬁes correctly and incorrectly generated policies with reasons, 
attaining a macro F1 score of 94.4 % (Section 5.2.3).
• We also evaluate AGentVLM in a real-world policy generation sce-
nario, showcasing its ability to handle intricate access control re-
quirements with multiple and hidden ACRs, which cannot be per-
formed by existing frameworks (Section 5.3).
• We release two annotated datasets, addressing the data scarcity of 
the access control domain [6,7] (Section 4).
• We make the implementation of AGentVLM and the developed 
datasets public via https://github.com/sakunaharinda/agentvlm.
2.  Related work
Previous research has proposed numerous automated policy gener-
ation frameworks [5–7,9–13,15,16,18,19,22] to identify NLACPs from 
high-level requirement speciﬁcation documents and extract their policy 
components. Xiao et al. proposed Text2Policy [15], based on seman-
tic pattern matching to identify NLACPs and shallow parsing based on 
four grammar rules to extract policy components from NLACPs. How-
ever, since it depends on a limited set of grammar rules, Text2Pol-
icy cannot identify and extract components from NLACPs that are not 
written according to the deﬁned rules [8]. Consequently, Text2Policy 
could extract policy components from only 34.4 % of NLACPs belong-
ing to Slankas et al.’s high-level requirement speciﬁcation documents 
[11]. Therefore, Slankas et al. proposed ACRE (Access Control Rela-
tion Extraction) [11,12,16,22] that employs dependency parsing with 
bootstrapping to extract policy components from NLACPs. Bootstrap-
ping helps ACRE to expand its set of dependency patterns (i.e., gram-
matical relationships between words of a sentence [8]) used to parse 
NLACPs dynamically, without being limited to a small number of pre-
deﬁned rules/patterns, as seen in Text2Policy [11]. However, despite at-
tempting to address some major limitations of Text2Policy, ACRE needs 
repetition of sentence structures throughout the high-level requirement 
speciﬁcations to perform reliably [6]. Furthermore, since it is still based 
on the grammatical structures of NLACPs, it may not be able to handle 
ambiguous and complex NLACPs [8].
Therefore, to improve the accuracy of access control policy genera-
tion without heavily relying on the grammatical structure of NLACPs, 
deep learning-based access control policy generation frameworks were 
developed [5–7,9,10,13,14,18,19,23]. For instance, Heaps et al. [13] in-
troduced a policy generation framework, using BERT (Bi-directional En-
coder Representation for Transformers) [24] for NLACP identiﬁcation, 
access type identiﬁcation, and policy components extraction from user 
stories [25]. However, since Heap et al.’s framework is based on named 
entity recognition (NER) for policy component extraction, it cannot han-
dle NLACPs with multiple subjects, actions, and resources, as it cannot 
identify which subject does which action to which resource [8,13]. As 
a solution, SRL-based frameworks that identify the predicate-argument 
structures of NLACPs (i.e., ACRs), indicating “who did what to whom” 
[26] and extract subjects (ARG0) and objects (ARG1) for given predi-
cates (V) [6] were developed. For example, Narouei et al. [6,9,10] and 
Xia et al. [7] developed frameworks based on SENNA (Semantic/syn-
tactic Extraction using a Neural Network Architecture) [27] and BERT-
based SRL models, respectively, to extract policy components. Never-
theless, existing SRL-based frameworks lack domain adaptation due to 
the scarcity of domain-related datasets [7,10,18]. Therefore, they either 
may not accurately extract access control policy components from each 
ACR of NLACPs (resulting in low recall) or may extract incorrect pol-
icy components associated with unrelated predicates (resulting in low 
precision), leading to low accuracy [7–9]. Furthermore, SRL only iden-
tiﬁes one subject and resource per each predicate of the NLACP [26]. 
Consequently, NLACPs with a subject containing multiple users (e.g., 
“The doctor and the nurse[𝑠𝑢𝑏𝑗𝑒𝑐𝑡 ] can read[𝑎𝑐𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛] the record[𝑟𝑒𝑠𝑜𝑢𝑟𝑐𝑒].”) need 
a technique like NER [6,9] or dependency parsing [7] to identify the 
users in the subject separately to derive its ACRs [6].
Even though the existing frameworks extract subjects, actions, and 
resources from NLACPs, they fall short when it comes to complex 
NLACPs containing ACRs with diﬀerent access decisions and intricate 
components, such as conditions and purposes [5–7,9–15,18,19,23]. As 
a result, policies generated using existing frameworks will allow ac-
cess requests, disregarding the purposes and conditions speciﬁed in the 
NLACPs. While this may aﬀect the compliance of policies with data pri-
vacy laws and regulations, such as GDPR and HIPAA, which focus on 
the purpose of data access, it may also result in insider attacks due to 
privilege misuse [4]. Additionally, existing frameworks [7,15] oﬀer an 
overall access decision for NLACPs, regardless of the individual access 
decisions made by the ACRs. That is because they exclusively depend on 
token classiﬁcation [6,7,9], which only extracts token/word-level infor-
mation in the NLACP (e.g., policy components), neglecting the semantic-
level information hidden in NLACPs’ meaning, like ACR decisions. In 
that case, if the NLACP has at least two ACRs with opposing access deci-
sions, existing frameworks always generate an incorrect access control 
policy [8].
Nonetheless, if an incorrect policy is generated due to the above lim-
itations or due to the complexities and ambiguities of NLACPs [6,9,13], 
feedback should be provided to the administrators with enough details 
to help them correct any errors [2,8,13,28]. However, none of the exist-
ing frameworks support this crucial step for handling incorrectly gener-
ated policies [6,7,9,11,13–15]. This remains a signiﬁcant gap that needs

3.  AGentVLM
AGentVLM is designed to improve the accuracy of the access con-
trol policy generation process, uniquely adapting LMs to generate access 
control policies in four steps, as shown in Fig. 1.
1. Step 1: Pre-processing
2. Step 2: NLACP Identiﬁcation
3. Step 3: Access Control Policy Generation
4. Step 4: Access Control Policy Veriﬁcation
According to Fig. 1, once the high-level requirement speciﬁcation 
document is provided as the input to the framework, it will ﬁrst be pre-
processed. In this research, we assume that the high-level requirement 
document does not contain any requirement conﬂicts following previ-
ous research [6,7,11]. However, it may contain NLACPs as well as sen-
tences that do not represent such requirements [6]. Therefore, as the 
second step, AGentVLM classiﬁes the pre-processed sentences of the in-
put document to identify NLACPs. Then, AGentVLM translates it into 
an access control policy containing ACRs with ﬁve policy components 
(i.e., subjects, actions, resources, purposes, and conditions) and their 
rule decisions (i.e., allow or deny) via a novel access control-speciﬁc 
structured information extraction technique in the third step. Unlike 
existing frameworks [6,7], the proposed policy generation technique in-
corporates token-level and semantic information of the NLACP to gener-
ate access control policies. This allows AGentVLM to generate granular 
access control policies from even complex NLACPs (e.g., NLACPs with 
implicit ACRs and multiple ACRs with multiple ACR decisions), a capa-
bility not supported in any other framework.
Nevertheless, the automatically generated access control policies still 
might not always be correct due to the limitations of LMs (or any ML 
model), such as inherent false positives and false negatives [6,13,29], 
hallucinations [30,31] or due to the complexities and ambiguities of the 
NLACPs [5]. Therefore, to identify such incorrectly generated policies 
and reﬁne them before applying them to the authorization system, in the 
fourth step, AGentVLM introduces a novel access control policy veriﬁer. 
It accepts an NLACP and the corresponding generated policy to decide 
whether or not the generated policy is correct. If the policy is classiﬁed 
as incorrect, the veriﬁer provides the reason (i.e., error type) for it being 
incorrect and the location of the error in the generated policy through 
XAI. This helps administrators easily ﬁnd erroneous ACRs and reﬁne 
them before adding them to the authorization system.
3.1.  Step 1: pre-processing
The input to AGentVLM is a conﬂict-free unstructured high-level re-
quirement speciﬁcation document written in NL [5,9]. Once the docu-
ment is provided, AGentVLM segments its paragraphs and resolves their 
coreferences, which has rarely been performed in existing frameworks 
[9,11].
Coreference resolution determines whether or not the two expres-
sions in a sentence or a paragraph refer to the same entity [6]. For 
instance, consider following NLACPs, “Doctors can read prescriptions. 
Also, they are allowed to write them.”. In the second NLACP, “they”
refers to “Doctors” and “them” refers to “prescriptions”, according to the 
ﬁrst NLACP. Therefore, once the coreferences are resolved, the sec-
ond NLACP will be improved into “Also, doctors are allowed to write 
prescriptions.”, allowing the framework to extract more meaningful and 
exclusive ACRs and generate policies.
Then, the paragraphs are segmented into sentences, and they are also 
tokenized to identify NLACPs in the second step.
3.2.  Step 2: NLACP identiﬁcation
High-level requirement speciﬁcation documents often contain sen-
tences that represent access control requirements (i.e., NLACP) as well 
as sentences that are not (i.e., non-NLACP) [6]. Therefore, as the second 
step, AGentVLM identiﬁes NLACPs to translate them into access control 
policies [6,7,9,13,15].

NLACP identiﬁcation is a text classiﬁcation task that classiﬁes a given 
sentence into NLACP or non-NLACP [6,7,9,11–13,16,22]. In recent 
years, for text classiﬁcation tasks like sentiment analysis, bi-directional 
transformer-based language models like BERT [24] and RoBERTa [32] 
have exhibited state-of-the-art performance on popular NLP benchmarks 
like GLUE [33]. In fact, BERT was used in previous research to identify 
NLACPs [7,13] more accurately compared to other traditional ML-based 
classiﬁcation techniques such as SVM [12] and k-NN [11]. Therefore, in 
AGentVLM, we also use BERT LM to identify NLACPs from NL sentences 
[7,13].
To this end, we add a feed-forward network (FFN) containing a sin-
gle linear layer to output the classiﬁcation result (i.e., NLACP or non-
NLACP) on top of the ﬁnal hidden state of BERT, corresponding to the 
[𝐶𝐿𝑆 ] token1. Then, we adapt the improved BERT LM to the access con-
trol domain by ﬁne-tuning it with the access control policy dataset con-
taining NLACPs and non-NLACPs (Section 4.1), minimizing the cross-
entropy loss [24]. As a result, it will be able to identify NLACPs from 
non-NLACPs accurately [7,13].
3.3.  Step 3: access control policy generation
After identifying NLACPs, AGentVLM translates them into access 
control policies (Fig. 1) using access control-speciﬁc structured information 
extraction. This process extracts information from an NLACP according 
to a deﬁned structure or hierarchy [20], delineating an access control 
policy. The deﬁned structure of an access control policy is shown in 
Fig. 2. It represents the underlying ACRs of the NLACP, access decisions, 
and policy components–such as subject, actions, resource, purpose, 
and condition–for each rule [11,18]. By providing the translation 
as an intermediate/structured representation of the NLACP, we enable 
organizations to transform AGentVLM’s output into their speciﬁc access 
control language (e.g., Access Control Lists, eXtensible Access Control 
Markup Language (XACML), etc.), regardless of the underlying model 
used in their authorization system (i.e., access control model agnostic) 
[8]. For example, AGentVLM translates the NLACP, “The business 
analysts should be able to read project requirements.” into {decision: 
allow; subject: business analyst; action: read; resource: 
project requirement; condition: none; purpose: none}, which 
can be easily transformed into XACML as shown in Appendix A.1 using 
a pre-deﬁned policy template. Using policy components to formulate 
machine-executable XACML policies using predeﬁned templates is a 
common and widely used practice in popular access control policy 
conﬁguration systems, such as WSO2 Identity Server [34].
Unlike existing frameworks, which can only extract token- or word-
level information [6,7,11], our proposed structured information extrac-
tion technique captures both token-level information (e.g., policy com-
ponents) and information hidden in the NLACP’s meaning (e.g., ACR 
decisions) at the same time. This results in granular access control poli-
cies, even when there are multiple ACRs with diﬀerent ACR decisions 
in the NLACP, a case that any existing framework cannot handle. For 
example, consider an NLACP, “The doctor can write prescriptions, but the 
nurse cannot.”. Given that NLACP, AGentVLM generates the access con-
trol policy as shown in Fig. 2, identifying policy components and ACR 
decisions based on the token-level and semantic properties of the NLACP 
[20].
Furthermore, our proposed access control-speciﬁc structured in-
formation extraction technique allows AGentVLM to identify implic-
it/hidden ACRs in an NLACP and generate access control policies. For
instance, in the example shown in Fig. 2, “…but  the nurse cannot” de-
notes an implicit ACR, as the action and the resource are not “explic-
itly” mentioned in relation to the subject “nurse” (e.g., “…but  the nurse 
cannot write prescriptions.”). Consequently, the policy generation frame-

works based only on token classiﬁcation (i.e., extracts only token-level

AGentVLM excels in identifying these nuances and generating policies 
for implicit ACRs.
To generate access control policies from NLACPs, previous research 
has often used general-purpose SRL models [6,7,9], leading to crucial 
limitations like extracting policy components for irrelevant actions due 
to the lack of domain adaptation [6,9]. In contrast, AGentVLM employs 
a Large LM (LLM) for access control policy generation after adapting 
it to the access control policy generation domain via ﬁne-tuning [20]. 
Such domain-adapted LLMs show high performance, especially in tasks 
that require both natural and domain-speciﬁc language, as well as an un-
derstanding of specialized terminology [6,35]. Consequently, they have 
also been successfully used in similar tasks to access control policy gen-
eration, like secure code generation from NL descriptions [30,35–38].
Among many powerful LLMs, we opt for LLaMa 3 8B, a state-of-the-
art, “small”, open-source LLM to generate access control policies from 
NLACPs [38,39]. The results show that LLaMa 3 demonstrates supe-
rior performance in popular benchmarks like MMLU (Multi-task Lan-
guage Understanding benchmark) [40] and HumanEval (Code genera-
tion benchmark) [41]. This makes LLaMa 3 the best open-source LLM 
available at the time of writing [38]. Furthermore, LLaMa 3 employs 
Grouped Query Attention [42], allowing it to focus on a group of to-
kens instead of every single token, improving the inference eﬃciency  
[38]. Therefore, by choosing LLaMa 3 8B, we ensure the smooth and ef-
ﬁcient implementation of AGentVLM, even on low-resource on-premise 
computers, while improving the accuracy of access control policy gen-
eration. First, this makes AGentVLM highly accessible to organizations, 
especially those with budget constraints [43]. Secondly, this safeguards 
the conﬁdentiality of access control requirements, as it eliminates the 
need to transmit them to proprietary black-box LLMs hosted in a diﬀer-
ent organization, like GPT-4, that can lead to data leakages [20,44,45].
We ﬁne-tune the LLaMa 3 8B using the access control policy dataset 
(Section 4.1) through Parameter Eﬃcient  Fine Tuning (PEFT) with low-
rank adapters (LoRA) [46], minimizing the negative log-likelihood of 
the generated access control policy [31]. This helps reduce catastrophic 
forgetting of LM ﬁne-tuning as it only tunes a small number of extra pa-
rameters of the LM, keeping the rest frozen [46]. Once the LLM is ﬁne-
tuned, AGentVLM becomes capable of generating access control poli-
cies from NLACPs, which are then subjected to automatic veriﬁcation in
Step 4.
3.4.  Step 4: access control policy veriﬁcation
When generating access control policies from NLACPs, LMs (or any 
ML model) can sometimes make incorrect generations. This can be due 
to several factors, such as hallucinations [30,31] and the complexities 
and ambiguities of the NLACPs [6,13]. Therefore, after the access con-
trol policy is generated, it should be veriﬁed to (1) check whether or 
not the generated policy is incorrect [30,47,48] and (2) warn adminis-
trators about errors of the incorrectly generated policy, allowing them 
to reﬁne it before adding it to the system [28]. This will help avoid ap-
plying incorrect policies to the authorization system and, in turn, reduce 
access control failures [13]. However, despite being crucial for accurate 
policy generation, none of the existing frameworks conduct this veriﬁ-
cation step. Therefore, by addressing this gap, in this step, we develop a 
novel access control policy veriﬁer that allows AGentVLM to verify the 
generated access control policy and provides feedback.

We develop AGentVLM’s veriﬁer as a multi-class sequence pair clas-
siﬁer that identiﬁes correct and incorrect policies with reasons. It pro-
vides a probability distribution over the 𝑚 = 12 classes based on the joint 
representation of the NLACP and the access control policy generated in 
Step 3. In other words, given the NLACP and its generated access con-
trol policy, AGentVLM decides whether the access control policy repre-
sents its NLACP (i.e., correct access control policy). On the other hand, 
if the policy does not represent the NLACP, AGentVLM identiﬁes the 
reason according to 11 error types belonging to two main categories: 
incorrect elements (i.e., incorrect decision, incorrect subject, incorrect 
actions, incorrect resource, incorrect purpose, and incorrect condition) 
and missing elements (i.e., missing ACR, missing subject, missing re-
source, missing purpose, and missing condition). After the classiﬁca-
tion, if the generated policy is identiﬁed as correct, AGentVLM outputs 
it, allowing it to be applied to the authorization system. Conversely, if 
the policy is classiﬁed under any of the 11 error types, it will be sent 
back to the administrator with the error type for further reﬁnements. 
sgr2 We use BART (Bidirectional and Auto-Regressive Transformers) LM 
[31,49] as the base model for AGentVLM’s veriﬁer. BART has demon-
strated superior capability in sequence pair classiﬁcation tasks such as 
textual entailment [49,50] and also in similar veriﬁcation tasks such as 
Python code veriﬁcation [51] and mathematical expressions veriﬁcation 
[31]. Therefore, we adapt BART for access control policy veriﬁcation 
by ﬁrst appending an FFN to its ﬁnal layer hidden states of the last de-
coder token, [eos] [31], to output the classiﬁcation results (i.e., correct 
or incorrect policy). Then, we ﬁne-tune it using the policy veriﬁcation 
dataset containing NLACPs and their correct and incorrect translations 
developed according to Section 4.2 by minimizing the cross-entropy loss 
[31]. At the inference time, it outputs the class that represents the cor-
rect access control policy if the NLACP and the generated policy match 
and also outputs a reason (i.e., the error type) otherwise. For example, 
if the generated access control policy for the NLACP “Employees are al-
lowed to submit expense claims with a receipt as proof for reimbursement.” is 
{decision: deny, subject: employee, action: submit, resource: expense claim, 
purpose: reimbursement, condition: none}, AGentVLM outputs the class be-
longs to “incorrect decision” as the error type, since the correct rule de-
cision of the NLACP should be allow.
Nevertheless, simply providing the error type as the feedback will not 
inform the administrator of how AGentVLM reached its decision and the 
features of the generated policy that inﬂuenced that decision. Such de-
tails may help administrators pinpoint the precise erroneous ACR when 
there are many ACRs in the incorrectly generated policy. To this end, 
AGentVLM utilizes integrated gradients-based feature attribution (FA) 
[52] to visualize features (i.e., tokens) of the generated access control 
policy that contributed to the veriﬁer’s decision (i.e., error type). We opt 
for a gradient-based FA technique instead of perturbation techniques 
due to its computational eﬃciency  and the availability of the veriﬁca-
tion model for gradient calculation [53,54]. Fig. 3 illustrates the veriﬁer 
input for the NLACP “Employees are allowed to submit expense claims with 
a receipt as proof for reimbursement.”, with tokens highlighted based on 
their attribution scores as determined by integrated gradients FA [54]. 
Notably, the token “deny” highlighted in green with the highest intensity 
indicates that it has the highest inﬂuence on the AGentVLM’s decision, 
“incorrect decision”. In other words, the administrator has to reﬁne the 
access decision of the incorrect NLACP from “deny” to “allow”, to make 
it correct. Providing that information together with the identiﬁed ex-
act error type will not only improve the explainability of AGentVLM, 
but also help administrators ﬁnd the location of the error and reﬁne the 
incorrect policy easily.
4.  Datasets
As mentioned in Section 3, we ﬁne-tune LMs utilized in AGentVLM 
using domain-related datasets to improve the accuracy of the process. 
In this section, we present those datasets.
4.1.  Access control policy dataset
The eﬀective adaptation of recent advancements of the NLP for ac-
cess control policy generation is restricted by the lack of domain-related 
datasets [7,14]. This is due to the conﬁdentiality of access requirements 
of organizations, except for a few projects that have made their access re-
quirements public [14]. However, the datasets based on such public ac-
cess requirements are either poorly annotated [7] or they are not enough 
for adapting LMs to access control policy generation. Therefore, to ad-
dress this gap, we developed a synthetic dataset based on ﬁve real-world 
high-level requirement speciﬁcation datasets introduced by Slankas
et al. [11].
The datasets introduced by Slankas et al. consist of sentences from 
high-level requirement speciﬁcations of three systems, namely iTrust, 
IBM course registration, and the CyberChair conference management 
[11]. Each sentence is ﬁrst categorized and labeled as NLACP (1) 
or non-NLACP (0). We call these annotations “sentence type anno-
tations”. Then, each NLACP is divided into its underlying ACRs and 
annotated by highlighting only three policy components: subject, ac-
tion, and resource. We call those annotations “entity annotations”. 
However, these annotations do not contain the access decision of 
each ACR of the NLACP [7], and also do not contain policy com-
ponents that provide contextual information to the NLACP, like pur-
poses and conditions [55]. As a result, the provided entity annotations

Therefore, we ﬁrst cleaned the data (i.e., removing duplicate entries 
and those with missing/corrupted sentences) and updated the annota-
tions with access decisions, purposes, and conditions based on previous 
research [55,56]. Then, we employed two popular data augmentation 
techniques: synonymy replacement using BERT embeddings and back 
translation [7], to generate additional NLACPs from the existing ones. 
For example, by replacing random words of the sentence “The doctor can 
read patient’s records.” with similar words using BERT embeddings [24], 
we can generate a sentence like “ER doctors can read patient’s medical his-
tory.”. Similarly, in back-translation, we translated the original NLACP 
from German to English and then back to German [57]. By doing so, we 
attempted to introduce variations to the sentences while maintaining the 
sentence structure, thereby helping LMs improve their generalization.
After generating synthetic data, we manually annotated them, rep-
resenting six components: subject, action, resource, purpose, condition, 
and access decision, separated into ACRs. In both annotation steps (up-
dating original dataset annotations and synthetic data annotation), if 
any of the above six components were absent in the sentence, the com-
ponent’s value was replaced with “none”. For example, the label of the 
NLACP from the iTrust T2P dataset, “The LHCP is able to view the com-
prehensive patient report.” looks like, “{decision: allow; subject: lhcp; ac-
tion: view; resource: comprehensive patient report; purpose: none; condi-
tion: none}”. Here, LHCP stands for “Licensed Health Care Professional”, 
a common role that can be found in the iTrust dataset. Furthermore, 
to support NLACP identiﬁcation, we labeled synthetic non-NLACP sen-
tences as 0 and NLACP sentences as 1, keeping the sentence type anno-
tations of Slankas et al.’s datasets unchanged.
The annotation process was primarily conducted by the lead author. 
After the lead author annotated the entire dataset, 20 % of the dataset 
was randomly selected and independently annotated by another cyber-
security researcher familiar with access control. The calculated Cohen 
Kappa value (𝜅 = 0.76) suggested that there was a “substantial agree-
ment” [58] between the annotators in the annotations. Most of the iden-
tiﬁed disagreements (30) were regarding exceptions and the condition 
parameter of the policy annotation. Then, we resolved the disagree-
ments between the annotators in a meeting and reﬁned the annotations 
of the entire dataset based on our discussion.
The summary of the resultant dataset in terms of the number of 
non-NLACP and NLACP sentences and the number of ACRs is shown in
Table 2. Cleaned document-folds indicate the information about data from 
Slankas et al.’s ﬁve real-world datasets after cleaning and updating an-
notations [11]. These document-folds do not contain any synthetic data. 
Following previous research, each fold is used to evaluate AGentVLM on 
real-world access requirements in diﬀerent steps described in Section 3, 
after training them with the rest of the data [6,7,9,11]. For example, 
we evaluate AGentVLM on CC after it is trained using the rest of the 
folds (i.e., CACP, IBM, T2P, and ACRE), and the synthetic data [14]. 
This helps evaluate the AGentVLM’s performance when it comes to out-
of-the-domain real-world access requirements, even though the training 
involved synthetic data [6]. The “Overall” dataset, which is a combina-
tion of all the document folds (i.e., real-world data) and synthetic data, 
is used to train and evaluate AGentVLM with a diverse set of NLACPs 
with random train (80 %) and test (20 %) sets.
4.2.  Access control policy veriﬁcation dataset
The access control policy veriﬁcation dataset is used to train and 
evaluate the access control policy veriﬁer discussed in Section 3.4. It 
classiﬁes a given sentence pair (i.e., NLACP and its generated access 
control policy in Step 3) into 12 categories, indicating whether the gen-
erated policy is correct or the reasons for it being incorrect. Therefore, to 
train the veriﬁer, we generated correct and incorrect policies for each 
NLACP of the access control policy dataset [31]. To this end, we fol-
lowed two approaches: model-based generation and policy component 
manipulation.
4.2.1.  Model-based generation
After ﬁne-tuning the LLaMa 3 LM using the access control policy 
dataset shown in Table 2, we used temperature sampling (𝑇 = 0.8) to 
generate ﬁve candidate policies (𝑛 = 5) for each NLACP of the training 
set [31]. Each unique generated policy for an NLACP is then labeled 
as correct if its ACRs and policy components are suﬃciently  matched 
with the ground truth labels. In the end, we have at least one correctly 
generated policy for each NLACP (including its ground truth annotation 
from the access control policy dataset) to create negative sequence pairs.
However, the ﬁne-tuned policy generation module exhibited high 
performance, yielding a signiﬁcantly larger proportion of correct poli-
cies compared to incorrect ones. Relying exclusively on model-based 
generation would therefore introduce severe class imbalance into the 
veriﬁcation dataset. This issue is exacerbated by the need to classify 
errors into 11 distinct types. Distributing the sparse model-generated 
errors across these categories would leave many error classes underrep-
resented relative to the “correct” class. Such an imbalance risks compro-
mising the veriﬁer’s ability to accurately identify speciﬁc error types. 
Therefore, to construct a balanced dataset with robust coverage across 
all error categories, we systematically manipulated the components of 
correctly generated policies to synthesize a diverse range of incorrect 
examples.

correctly generated policies and ground-truth access control policies for 
each NLACP according to four techniques [31]. They are (a) Decision 
change. Randomly change the decision of an ACR from allow to deny

5.2.  Experiment results
This section presents the evaluation results of AGentVLM and com-

5.2.1.  NLACP Identiﬁcation
As shown in Table 3, AGentVLM achieves an average document fold

5.2.2.  Access control policy generation
When evaluating the access control policy generation performance, 
we ﬁrst evaluate how eﬀectively AGentVLM extracts “subjects”, “re-
sources”, “purposes”, and “conditions” for each “action” in NLACPs, fol-
lowing the previous research [6,7,9]. The obtained results are reported 
in Table 4 under two settings of AGentVLM: SAR and SARCP. SAR set-
ting only considers subjects and resources for a given action to calculate 
the F1 score, facilitating the comparison with current state-of-the-art 
[6,7,9,11,12]. On the other hand, the SARCP setting extends the eval-
uation to include purposes and conditions with subjects and resources 
when computing the F1 score for each action of NLACPs.
However, since we corrected and updated the entity annotations for 
document folds (Section 4.1), we cannot compare AGentVLM’s perfor-
mance evaluated using the updated datasets with the current state-of-
the-art evaluated through the old datasets (as reported in their respec-
tive papers). Therefore, ﬁrst, we implement the two most reliable policy 
generation frameworks [6,7] from scratch, as not only are their imple-
mentations not publicly available, but also the authors of those frame-
works were unreachable. After implementation, we evaluate their per-
formances on our improved datasets shown in Table 2 and compare 
them with AGentVLM. It is worth mentioning that we do not need to 
train the implemented frameworks, as they rely on pre-trained general-
purpose SRL models without domain adaptation to extract policy com-
ponents [6,7,9].
According to Table 4, AGentVLM outperforms the existing state-of-
the-art policy generation frameworks by at least 34.4 % (i.e., absolute 
improvement) on average in the SAR setting [7]. Additionally, it yields 
an average F1 score of 73.0±1.4 % with 95 % bootstrap CI of [71.9, 74.1] 
over the document folds and 80.6±1.8 % with 95 % bootstrap CI of [79.3, 
82.0] on the “Overall” dataset, considering policy components such as 
purposes and conditions [55], in addition to basic subjects, actions, 
and resources in the SARCP setting. Since the CIs reported for all the 
document folds consistently lie entirely above the strongest baseline’s 
policy component extraction performances, the improvement gained 
by AGentVLM is statistically robust. This demonstrates that AGentVLM 
can translate access control policies, even those with complex policy
components and linguistic forms [55], with a higher accuracy compared 
to existing frameworks.

5.2.3.  Access control policy veriﬁcation
In order to train and evaluate AGentVLM’s access control policy ver-
iﬁer, we utilize the access control policy veriﬁcation dataset developed 
using “Overall” dataset as described in Section 4.2. We speciﬁcally use 
the “Overall” dataset, as it contains a wide variety of access control re-
quirements from diﬀerent domains, including healthcare (data from the 
T2P and ACRE datasets), conference management (data from the CC 
dataset), and education (data from the IBM dataset).
After developing the veriﬁcation dataset, we randomly divide it into 
training (80 %), validation (10 %), and test (10 %) sets to train the ver-
iﬁer, select the best checkpoint based on the validation set, and test 
the veriﬁer with the selected checkpoint. The results from the testing 
stage are reported in Table 6. According to Table 6, AGentVLM’s veriﬁer 
identiﬁes correct and incorrect access control policies with F1 scores of

successfully identiﬁes each error category with an F1 score greater than 
76 %.
5.3.  Real-world use cases
As reported in Sections 5.2.1–5.2.3, AGentVLM signiﬁcantly outper-
forms the current state-of-the-art, improving the accuracy of the access 
control policy generation process. This performance was achieved on 
the out-of-domain data from ﬁve high-level requirement speciﬁcation 
datasets. As we mentioned in Section 5.2, the original sentences of those 
datasets were extracted by Slankas et al. from three real-world systems, 
namely iTrust, CyberChair, and IBM course registration, through de-
pendency parsing [11,12]. However, during the dataset preparation, 
Slankas et al. reﬁned these sentences using techniques like replacing 
shorthand and removing text that is diﬃcult  to process by their frame-
work [12]. As a result, the evaluation of AGentVLM conducted on these 
datasets in Section 5 may not accurately reﬂect its real-world perfor-
mance. Therefore, we applied AGentVLM to real-world privacy policies 
from two websites, namely HotCRP and Amazon Web Services (AWS). 
Instead of using access control requirements, we opted for privacy poli-
cies because of (1) the unavailability of the conﬁdential organization-
speciﬁc access control requirements and (2) the similarities in policy 
components between privacy and access control policies [55,56].

policies and reformatted it in markdown format for ease of processing 
and visualization. Then, AGentVLM ﬁrst pre-processed the reformat-
ted document using the techniques described in Section 3.1. After pre-
processing, it correctly identiﬁed 26 out of 28 NLACPs on the HotCRP

AGentVLM, if it is not resolving coreferences. However, after adding 
coreference resolution as one of AGentVLM’s pre-processing techniques, 
it correctly identiﬁed “Rather, HotCRP.com service broker that data on be-
half of scholarly societies …”  as an NLACP.
After the NLACP identiﬁcation step, AGentVLM then generates 
access control policies from the identiﬁed NLACPs as described in
Section 3.3. As we found out, AGentVLM discovers complex and im-
plicit/hidden ACRs within NLACPs and generates access control policies 
accurately. For example, AGentVLM generated the access control policy 
for the NLACP “Demographic data is stored in user global proﬁles, and can 
only be modiﬁed by users (never by site managers).” as,
AGentVLM identiﬁed that the “site manager” is not allowed to “mod-
ify” “demographic data” even though it is not mentioned in the NLACP 
explicitly. In contrast, once we provided the same NLACP to the state-of-
the-art policy generation framework by Xia et al. [7], it only identiﬁed 
the subject as “by users by site managers” and the resource as “demo-
graphic data” for the action “modify”. Not only did it not extract sub-
jects “user” and “site manager” separately, but it also could not iden-
tify there are two ACRs with opposite access decisions. As a result, Xia 
et al.’s framework achieved only a 32.8 % F1 score in extracting pol-
icy components from privacy policies, compared to 96.5 % attained by 
AGentVLM2.
Finally, AGentVLM utilized the joint representation of the NLACP 
and its generated access control policy to verify the generated policy 
with respect to the NLACP as detailed in Section 3.4. After manual in-
spection, we found that AGentVLM identiﬁes correct and incorrect poli-
cies generated for HotCRP privacy policies with an accuracy of 88 %.
5.3.2.  Use case 2: AWS
We followed the same steps mentioned in Section 5.3.1 to prepare 
the AWS privacy notice and provided it as the input to AGentVLM. We 
extracted 86 sentences, and 46 of them were NLACPs. AGentVLM identi-
ﬁed NLACPs from the preprocessed sentences with an F1 score of 89.8 % 
and extracted policy components from NLACPs, achieving an F1 score 
of 90.9 %. In the ﬁnal veriﬁcation stage, AGentVLM obtained the veri-
ﬁcation F1 score of 88 % and an accuracy of 89 % (41/46). Speciﬁcally, 
AGentVLM accurately classiﬁed 31 out of 33 correctly generated policies 
as “correct” and correctly identiﬁed 10 out of 13 errors of the generated 
policies.
It is worth noting that, in both use cases, veriﬁcation has yielded 
similar accuracies (88 % and 89 %), which are substantially lower than 
the 97.1 % reported on our held-out test set (Table 6). This discrepancy 
can be explained by two factors. First, AGentVLM’s veriﬁer is trained 
with natural language access requirements and their correct/incorrect 
2 We conducted the same annotation process mentioned in Section 4.1 for 
labeling the NLACPs as ground truths
translations. As a result, it specializes in identifying errors in access 
control policies. When applied to a diﬀerent domain, such as privacy
notices, whose input distribution (e.g., vocabulary, phrasing, and con-
textual cues) diﬀers from the training data, its performance naturally de-
grades due to domain shift [62]. This behavior is consistent with prior 
ﬁndings in NLP, where models ﬁne-tuned on a speciﬁc domain often 
struggle to generalize when the underlying data distribution changes 
[62]. Second, after careful analysis of the classiﬁcation reports of the 
predictions for use cases, we observed a signiﬁcant class imbalance in 
the veriﬁcation inputs. The input to AGentVLM’s veriﬁer contains the 
joint representations of NLACPs and their translations, generated in the 
access control policy generation step, allowing the veriﬁer to classify 
the translations are either correct or incorrect according to 11 error 
categories (Section 3.4). However, in the AWS use case, for example, 
33 instances had the correct translation, while only one instance con-
tained an incorrect resource in the translation. Similarly, there was only 
one instance of incorrect action, one instance of incorrect decision, one 
instance of missing purpose, three instances of missing rules, and six 
instances of missing condition. Thus, the ratio of correct to incorrect 
instances was 33 to 13. This imbalance arises because AGentVLMgen-
erates a relatively high proportion of correct policies (F1 = 90.9 %), 
leaving few examples of rare error types for the veriﬁer to predict. As 
a result, a single misclassiﬁcation in these underrepresented categories, 
such as an incorrect resource, may lead to a 0 or a low F1 score, which in 
turn disproportionately lowers the overall veriﬁcation F1 score. By con-
trast, the test set contains ≈ 100 instances per almost all the categories, 
making its class distribution much more uniform. In such settings, the 
impact of any single misclassiﬁcation is attenuated, yielding a higher 
aggregate F1 score (Table 6). A similar eﬀect is observed for accuracy: 
with 1184 entries in the test set, one error changes accuracy by only 
0.08 %, whereas in the AWS policy (46 entries), one error reduces ac-
curacy by 2.2 %. This sensitivity to small sample sizes further ampliﬁes 
the apparent performance gap between in-domain and out-of-domain 
evaluations.
6.  Discussion
In this paper, we introduce AGentVLM, a novel access control pol-
icy generation and veriﬁcation framework based on LMs, improving 
the accuracy of the access control policy generation process. It iden-
tiﬁes access control requirements from high-level requirement speciﬁ-
cations and translates them into access control policies using a novel 
access control-speciﬁc structured information extraction technique. Fi-
nally, AGentVLM veriﬁes the correctness of the generated policies us-
ing a novel access control policy veriﬁcation method, providing feed-
back that highlights errors in them. Based on this veriﬁcation feedback, 
AGentVLM introduces a way to incorporate human administrators in the 
loop to reﬁne incorrect policies that might be generated due to inherent 
complexities of NLACPs [2]. Compared to less reliable, fully automated 
policy generation frameworks [6,7,11,15], this human-in-the-loop ap-
proach can improve the real-world accuracy and security of the access 
control policy generation process.
By utilizing small open-source LMs, we enable the eﬃcient  imple-
mentation of AGentVLM in local computers even without high comput-
ing power. This helps preserve the conﬁdentiality of the organization’s 
access control requirements while improving the accuracy of policy gen-

eration. Our extensive evaluation suggests that AGentVLM outperforms 
all the existing state-of-the-art policy generation frameworks [6,7,9–12] 
in every step of the access control policy generation by signiﬁcant
margins.
NLACP identiﬁcation. As reported in Section 5.2.1, AGentVLM 
identiﬁes NLACPs from high-level requirement speciﬁcations with an 
average document-fold F1 score of 90.6 % outperforming the state-of-
the-art frameworks [7,11,12,16,18] by at least 5.4 %. This superior per-
formance of AGentVLM can mainly be attributed to the BERT LM’s abil-
ity to incorporate context from each token of the sentence through the 
self-attention mechanism when classifying it as NLACP or non-NLACP. 
As previous research also pointed out, such contextual information ex-
tracted from dependencies between words/tokens of a sentence is im-
perative in text classiﬁcation [63]. Consequently, AGentVLM is able to 
identify NLACPs signiﬁcantly better compared to other frameworks that 
employ traditional ML techniques like k-NN [6,11,12].
Access control policy generation. As we showed in Section 5.2.2, 
AGentVLM extracts policy components from identiﬁed NLACPs with an 
average F1 score of 73.0 %, outperforming the existing frameworks by at 
least 30.6 % (absolute improvement). Interestingly, AGentVLM achieves 
such a signiﬁcant improvement even when extracting purposes and con-
ditions, in addition to subjects, actions, and resources, that cannot be 
supported by any of the state-of-the-art policy generation frameworks 
[6,7,9,18]. This is particularly important if AGentVLM is used to gen-
erate Purpose-based Access Control (PBAC) policies that grant/deny ac-
cess based on the “purpose” of data access to comply with data privacy 
laws and regulations like GDPR [64]. Therefore, compared to the current 
state-of-the-art, AGentVLM enables organizations to generate granular 
access control policies [55].
The main reason for this AGentVLM’s high policy component extrac-
tion/rule generation accuracy compared to the current state-of-the-art 
is its high precision [6,7,9,11] achieved via domain adaptation, which 
is lacking in existing frameworks. The existing state-of-the-art frame-
works (based on SRL) [6,7], often extract policy components for actions 
that are not related to the current access control policy [6,7,9,10]. For 
instance, consider the NLACP with two verbs, “The doctor can read pa-
tient’s records to prescribe medicine”. Even though there are two verbs, the 
NLACP contains only one ACR with the subject “doctor”, action “read”, 
resource “patient’s record”, and purpose “prescribe medicine” [55,56]. 
However, given this NLACP, existing frameworks based on SRL will 
identify two ACRs for each verb as they are not adapted to the access 
control domain to identify “actions” in the presence of purposes and 
conditions that have complex linguistic structures [8,55,56]. Therefore, 
when evaluating the framework’s ability to extract subjects, actions, 
and resources, the extracted components from the ACR with the ac-
tion “prescribe” will be considered as false positives [56]. Even though 
Xia et al. used a dictionary [15] to store those actions not related to 
the access control policy [7] and ﬁlter out unwanted ACRs (semantic 
role labels in this case), they are not ﬂexible enough to handle all such 
cases. Consequently, those false positives are harder to avoid, resulting 
in low precision, leading to signiﬁcantly lower F1 scores [6] compared 
to AGentVLM. Additionally, AGentVLM outperforms much larger LLMs 
like GPT-4o in access control rule generation (Table 5), further demon-
strating the importance of domain adaptation.
Moreover, existing automated policy generation frameworks are 
based on token classiﬁcation, which assigns a label to each token in 
the provided NLACP, representing policy components such as subjects, 
actions, and resources. As a result, they can extract policy components 
that are explicitly mentioned in the NLACP. However, in complex cases, 
policy components may not be explicitly mentioned in the NLACPs (e.g., 
in exceptions), similar to the ACR decisions (i.e., allow/deny). They 
should be inferred from the broader semantic context rather than from 
explicit tokens of the NLACP, a capability that is not available in token 
classiﬁcation-based state-of-the-art frameworks [6,7,65]. For example, 
in the HotCRP use case (Section 5.3.1), the NLACP “Demographic data is 
stored in user global proﬁles, and can only be modiﬁed by users (never by 
site managers).” does not explicitly state that the site managers are not 
allowed to access demographic data. Nevertheless, AGentVLM correctly 
extracted policy components from that ACR with its eﬀect/access de-
cision as “deny”. Similarly, AGentVLM also correctly identiﬁes ACRs, 
their individual rule decisions, and policy components from exceptions 
like “LHCPs, except nurses can read patient records.” In contrast, exist-
ing frameworks fail to identify such implicit ACRs and their decisions 
that are not completely represented by explicit tokens, misrepresenting 
important NLACPs such as exceptions.
AGentVLM’s this capability is enabled by its access control-speciﬁc 
structured information extraction technique, which focuses on ex-
tracting token-level explicit information as well as information hid-
den in NLACP’s meaning. The resultant intermediate representations 
of NLACPs are access control-model agnostic, allowing organizations 
with heterogeneous authorization infrastructures to adopt the system. 
However, while it can be automatically transformed into access con-
trol languages like XACML via templates in most cases, it may not 
be trivial when representing extracted “condition” in such languages 
for Attribute-based Access Control (ABAC) policies. Speciﬁcally, when 
ABAC policies are represented in XACML, they require conditions to be 
expressed using organization-speciﬁc attribute identiﬁers, data types, 
and predeﬁned functions. Consequently, accurate translation depends 
on the availability of external schemas and ontologies that deﬁne how 
natural-language concepts (e.g., “patient’s age” or “business hours”) cor-
respond to system attributes. These mappings are inherently domain and 
deployment-speciﬁc and cannot be reliably inferred in a generic setting. 
As a result, AGentVLM currently treats extracted conditions as a se-
mantic, machine-processable intermediate form, rather than as ready-to-
deploy XACML constructs. Therefore, as future work, we will utilize Re-
trieval Augmented Generation (RAG) [66] to incorporate organization-
speciﬁc attribute deﬁnitions, thereby facilitating the direct translation 
of all the components AGentVLM extracts into XACML-like policies.
Access control policy veriﬁcation. According to Table 6, 
AGentVLM identiﬁes correct and incorrect policies with F1 scores of 
90.6 % and 98.3 %, respectively, achieving the average macro F1 score 
of 94.4 %. It shows that AGentVLM not only generates policies reliably 
but also accurately identiﬁes its own mistakes. However, indicating only 
whether or not the generated policy is incorrect may not provide suﬃ-
cient information to administrators that is necessary for reﬁning it cor-
rectly, often leading them to trial and error practices [28]. As previous 
research found out, these practices might introduce more errors to the 
policy, leading to access control failures, in the end, [28]. Therefore, 
AGentVLM avoids such negative outcomes by providing detailed feed-
back indicating the exact reason for the generated policy being incor-
rect (identiﬁed with an F1 score of more than 76 %) and also explaining 
where the error is located in the generated policy through feature at-
tribution. Consequently, AGentVLM further enhances the accuracy of 
policy generation through explainable artiﬁcial intelligence (XAI) tech-
niques, while reducing access control failures resulting from mistakes 
made by the automated policy generation process.
This research developed AGentVLM, focusing on accurately trans-
lating provided natural language access requirements into policies. Our 
evaluation, therefore, centers on correctness of the generated policies. 
We demonstrated that the AGentVLM’s design improves the correctness
compared to existing approaches, as evidenced by F1 scores, the metric 
used in previous related work [6,7]. As recognized in the access con-
trol literature, however, correctness alone does not fully capture policy 
quality [67]. High-quality policies also depend on additional properties, 
including consistency (absence of conﬂicting rules), minimality (lack of 
redundant rules), relevance (rules pertain only to appropriate subject-
s/actions), and completeness (coverage of all required access scenarios) 
[67]. Although the above properties (except correctness [67]) largely de-
pend on the high-level requirement speciﬁcations (the input provided 
to AGentVLM), evaluating them helps identify holes/mistakes in the in-
put document and provides feedback to policymakers who create them, 
which is out of scope of this research.

Furthermore, an eﬀective policy quality assessment requires com-
plete and contextually rich high-level requirement speciﬁcations, which 
are rarely available [8]. For instance, while we evaluated AGentVLM 
with datasets containing real-world access requirements extracted by 
Slankas et al. [11], these datasets are pre-processed and incomplete (i.e., 
contain missing access requirements). Consequently, since AGentVLM 
relies on the provided input and translates it into access control poli-
cies, the generated policies will not cover all possible access scenarios 
(i.e., completeness property) due to the incompleteness of the input. This 
also applies when evaluating whether generated policies are minimal, 
consistent, or relevant, without having access to additional contextual 
information and complete speciﬁcation documents. Therefore, in future 
works, we will utilize Binary Decision Diagrams (BDD) [68] to evaluate 
the generated policies from a complete high-level requirement speciﬁ-
cation document, thereby ensuring their quality.
Safeguards against adversarial attacks. The LMs/LLMs employed 
by AGentVLM, especially LLaMa 3 used for policy generation, have un-
dergone safety pretraining and extensive adversarial testing via Red 
Teaming [39]. We further ﬁne-tune them using cleaned public datasets to 
reliably carry out the steps described in Section 3. As a result, AGentVLM 
is equipped to withstand several attacks described on [36], like extrac-
tion and inference attacks that are designed to steal sensitive informa-
tion and insights from its training data, respectively. Furthermore, each 
LM/LLM in AGentVLM is responsible for performing a single task (either 
NLACP identiﬁcation or policy generation) from speciﬁc pre-processed 
inputs. The outputs are automatically post-processed, veriﬁed, and man-
ually reﬁned (if necessary) before being added to the authorization sys-
tem. These precautions taken throughout the policy generation process 
make AGentVLM also robust against attacks such as instruction tuning 
attacks like prompt injections that manipulate the input to the LLM 
to generate undesirable outputs [36]. If an adversary provides a non-
NLACP input, it will be rejected by the NLACP identiﬁcation step at the 
beginning. Even if the input bypasses the identiﬁcation step, the gener-
ated output from the policy generation step will fail the post-processing 
stage, resulting in an empty access control policy “{}”. Finally, the veriﬁ-
cation step will alert the administrators about the incorrect/undesirable 
output, allowing them to reﬁne or reject the output before adding it to 
the system.
7.  Conclusion and future work
Manually generating the initial set of access control policies from 
high-level requirement speciﬁcations is a laborious and error-prone pro-
cess [6,7,9]. Consequently, system administrators sometimes make mis-
takes when generating access control policies, potentially leading to 
data breaches due to access control failures [1,69]. Even though pre-
vious research introduced fully automated frameworks to help adminis-
trators in this manual policy generation process, their limitations, such 
as their inability to handle complex access requirements, render them 
unreliable [6,7,9,11,13,15]. Therefore, to address such limitations, this 
paper proposes AGentVLM, a novel access control policy generation and 
veriﬁcation framework, enhancing the accuracy of the access control 
policy generation process using transformer-based LMs. Our experimen-
tal results show that AGentVLM accurately identiﬁes NLACPs from high-
level requirement speciﬁcations and translates them into access control 
policies, signiﬁcantly improving the accuracy of access control policy 
generation. In contrast to existing frameworks, AGentVLM translates 
complex access control requirements containing multiple ACRs with 
even intricate components like purposes and conditions, in addition to 
subjects, actions, and resources. To this end, we introduce a novel access 
control-speciﬁc structured information extraction technique. Based on 
this technique, we show that AGentVLM can successfully extract hidden 
ACRs from NLACPs and generate access control policies by taking the 
access decisions of each ACR into account, through real-world use cases. 
Finally, we introduce AGentVLM’s novel automatic policy veriﬁcation 
mechanism that veriﬁes the generated policies and informs system ad-
ministrators on generation errors via XAI, addressing a signiﬁcant gap 
in access control policy generation research [13,28]. In addition to these 
technical contributions, we also publicly release two annotated datasets, 
addressing the data scarcity in the domain [6,7].
As future work, we intend to improve the feedback sent to the system 
administrator if the generated policy is found to be incorrect. This is im-
portant as the details that should be included in the feedback may need 
to diﬀer based on the system administrator’s level of experience. For 
instance, while an experienced system administrator may easily iden-
tify the error and its location, and reﬁne the incorrect policy accurately, 
a less experienced one may need more specialized step-by-step guid-
ance on reﬁning the policy [28,70]. If not, they will resort to trial-and-
error practices, introducing more errors into the policy. Therefore, we 
will employ explainable security (XSec) concepts (i.e., Why, Who, What, 
Where, When, and How) [71] to design the feedback through a user study 
with system administrators of varying expertise levels, ensuring that the 
feedback message provides clearer and more detailed information to 
them. It will help all administrators accurately interpret the feedback 
and reﬁne the incorrect policy irrespective of their experience, further 
strengthening the accuracy of access control policy generation [28]. Sec-
ondly, we will use the administrator’s response to the AGentVLM’s feed-
back to iteratively improve the accuracy of AGentVLM via reinforce-
ment learning [72–74]. Finally, we will empirically evaluate AGentVLM 
through a lab study to investigate how its theoretical accuracy reﬂects 
in real-world policy generation scenarios [55,75]. To this end, we will 
ﬁrst develop a usable policy conﬁguration interface based on Nielsen’s 
usability quality components [76] and iteratively improve it through 
contextual design [77]. Then, we will present AGentVLM to administra-
tors through the developed interface, asking them to perform realistic 
policy generation tasks [75,78]. During the study, we will record their 
think-aloud data, time taken to perform each task, errors made by them, 
etc., and evaluate their subjective satisfaction using evaluation metrics 
like the System Usability Scale (SUS) through interviews [79]. Beyond 
dataset-based evaluation, this will help investigate how system admin-
istrators perceive automated policy generation systems for real-world 
policy generation goals (e.g., how useful are the automatically gener-
ated policies in real-world?) and how the usability of such systems con-
tributes to improving the overall accuracy, eﬃciency,  and trust of the 
automated policy generation process.
