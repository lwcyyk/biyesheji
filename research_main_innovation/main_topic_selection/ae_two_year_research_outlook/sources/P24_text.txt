Abstract
A key challenge in digital credential systems is revocation,
that is, the ability to invalidate credentials post-issuance and
verify their status upon presentation. While anonymous cre-
dentials enhance privacy over classical credentials (e.g., by
providing unlinkability), they complicate revocation. Existing
revocation schemes for anonymous credentials often suffer
from high client or verifier computation, long delays before
revocation takes effect (e.g., epoch-based settings), or require
updates to all users with each revocation.
We present an efficient, real-time revocation system for anony-
mous credentials with decentralized revocation authorities
based on a novel primitive calledPrivate Record Certification
(PRC). PRC enables users to obtain a certificate for a record
stored in a server-managed database without the servers learn-
ing which record was requested. This primitive is of indepen-
dent interest, and we construct it by combining techniques
from private information retrieval and secure multi-party com-
putation. Our revocation scheme outsources its computation
to the revocation authorities and has minimal overhead for
clients and verifiers, while ensuring the communication costs
are sublinear in the number of credentials. We build a proto-
type and demonstrate that our system achieves sub-second
real-time latency for PRC requests, at a scale of over1 billion
credentials, with an online operational cost of 2.5$ per server
for processing1 millionPRC requests.
1 Introduction
As governments increasingly move public services online,
electronic identification (eID) is becoming a critical national
infrastructure, serving as the entry point for essential services
like taxes, healthcare, mobility, and education. While eID sys-
tems ease administrative burdens for citizens, they also raise
concerns about mass surveillance and the erosion of funda-
mental rights, such as privacy. European regulations [39] call
for privacy-preserving digital identities that provide strong
authenticity without enabling cross-context linkability. The
European Digital Identity (EUDI) Wallet initiative seeks to
realize these legal requirements, but has major shortcomings
and does not yet provide the demanded privacy guarantees [6].
Anonymous credentials [20, 22, 23] have long been known
to achieve this securely and efficiently and future EUDIW
iterations may adopt anonymous credentials [38].
A central challenge in credential systems isrevocation: the
ability to invalidate credentials after issuance, and to verify
a credential’s status during presentation. Privacy-preserving
revocation is not a technical nicety; it is a two-sided safe-
guard. It protects citizens against identity theft, lost creden-
tials, misuse, and surveillance, while empowering authorities
to enforce accountability in a rights-respecting manner. For in-
stance, suspended driver’s licenses or fraudulent professional
certifications must be revoked without enabling pervasive
profiling of citizens. Privacy can be important for revocation
authorities too, especially when they are law enforcement or
government agencies (e.g., for driver’s licenses). Solutions
that rely on public revocation lists disclose the status of all cre-
dentials, exposing unnecessary data and creating legal risks.
Demonstrating that strong privacy and real-time accountabil-
ity can be achieved simultaneously is essential to counter
the false trade-off between security and civil liberties, which
often stalls adoption of privacy-preserving solutions.
While anonymous credentials offer strong privacy, they
complicate revocation. Existing revocation schemes [5,14,16,
18, 21, 68, 74, 81] incur expensive client updates per revoca-
tion, high verifier costs, or delayed revocation via time-based
epochs, which create “opportunity windows” for abuse of
revoked credentials.
We study the requirements of a practical revocation scheme.
Viable systems should: 1) scale for nation-wide deploy-
ment, supporting hundreds of millions of credentials, 2)
support real-time revocation that eliminates the abuse win-
dow, and 3) achieve low latency with 4) minimal computa-
tional/communication overhead for clients and verifiers, en-
abling deployment on mobile devices and constrained net-
works. We align revocation privacy requirements with those
of anonymous credential presentations. Since revocation in-

troduces authorities absent from classic credentials, we also

which we calldatabase privacy. In particular, clients must
prove ownership of a credential to learn its revocation status.
We construct a revocation scheme for anonymous creden-
tials that achieves scalability, efficiency, and real-time revoca-
tion while maintaining strong privacy guarantees. Clients ob-
tain a non-revocation token from a Revocation Authority (RA)
and present it, along with their credential, to verifiers. We tar-
get an on-demand scenario where clients request a new token
immediately before each credential presentation. Therefore,
we must guarantee that non-revocation queries remain unlink-
able. We require similar privacy guarantees forRAs as we due
for issuers and verifiers in credential systems. This is needed,
as otherwise the RA could track or discriminate against users
based on their usage patterns, leaked attributes, or revocation
status. To achieve this guarantee, we assume multiple RAs
that share a replicated database in an anytrust setting, where
unlinkability is preserved as long as at least one RA remains
honest. The client does not need to know which one is honest.
This is a realistic model, as in practice, the distributedRA can
be realized as a consortium of different servers, e.g., operated
by different governmental and non-governmental institutions.
As a first step toward a revocation scheme, we introduce a
new primitive called Private Record Certification (PRC) that
enables clients to privately obtain a certificate for a record in a
server-managed database. Concretely, it provides a signature
on a recordD [idx] together with its index idx, while hiding
the identity of the client, the index itself, and the record value.
Clients can use this signature in standard zero-knowledge
proofs to demonstrate their record satisfies a desired property.
Beyond revocation, PRC is of independent interest for appli-
cations such as privacy-preserving proofs of credit score or
account balance. Our PRC construction combines techniques
from Private Information Retrieval (PIR) [25,33] with Secure
Multi-Party Computation ( MPC) [44]. Specifically, we
introduce a novel conversion from Boolean secret sharing
to One-Hot Encoding (OHE) achieving theoretically minimal
client-side complexity, logarithmic in the number of database
records (i.e., the number of bits needed to represent the index
in cleartext). We then combine our secret sharing operations
in a new way with homomorphic commitments, enabling
a seamless transition to asymmetric operations needed for
building a non-revocation token via a (threshold) signature.

1.1 Technical Overview
We provide a high-level technical overview of our solution
next. We build a revocation scheme where a decentralized
RA keeps a private status listD, such thatD [idx]∈ {0,1} is
the validity status of the credential with a private index idx
(see §6). When clients want to present one of their credentials,
they need to prove thatD [idx] =1 , while 1) binding idx to the
index embedded in their credential and 2) hidingidx to ensure
unlinkability. To achieve this goal, we define a primitive called
PRC that allows the client to interact with RAs to privately
obtain a signed commitment linking (idx,D[idx]), also called
a non-revocation token. When clients present their credential,
they present a non-revocation token and use zero-knowledge
proofs to link the index in the credential to the token (i.e.,
proof of discrete log equality) and reveal the record value
D[idx] =1 . The verifier validates a) the credential presenta-
tion, b) the RA’s signature on the token, and c) the ZK-proof
proving the token corresponds to the credential. Since PRC
providesunforgeability, the verifier can be certain that the
revealed revocation status belongs to presented credential.
PRC.We define and construct a PRC scheme in §5, where
given a private index, the PRC servers compute and then sign
a commitment to (D[idx],idx) that binds an index to its record
value. We design a PRC protocol in the following steps: 1)
the client Boolean secret shares its index among PRC servers.
2) the servers convert their boolean shares ofidx to arithmetic
shares and compute arithmetic shares of the database record
D[idx]. This step has a similar nature as PIR, and we design
a custom optimized variant of it, which we discuss below. 3)
Each server commits to its arithmetic share of the index and
record value via a homomorphic commitment like Pedersen.
4) The PRC servers aggregate the homomorphic commitment.
As we deliberately choose the same group order between our
commitment and arithmetic sharing, this aggregation acts as a
secret reconstruction without revealing the cleartext values to
PRC servers. 5) The PRC servers sign the final commitment.
We extend this scheme in §5.4 to achieve database privacy,
by requiring clients to add a ZK-proof (similar to the proof
used for presenting a non-revocation token) showing that they
have an authorization for the requested index.
Record retrieval.In step 2) of our PRC scheme, the PRC
servers - each holding a replicated databaseD- obtain an arith-
metic sharing of the recordD[idx], where idx is the client’s pri-
vate index. This step resembles multi-serverPIR schemes [25]
which (Boolean) secret share the OHE of the client’s index,
an all-zero vector with a single 1 at position idx. The servers

2 Related Work
Anonymous credential revocation.A prominent revoca-
tion approach for anonymous credentials relies on dynamic
accumulators [5, 18, 21, 68], which allow users to prove
(non-)membership in an accumulator representing the set of
all revoked or valid credentials. An inherent drawback is that
each accumulator update (e.g., upon revocation) invalidates
existing witnesses, forcing users to either update their witness
for every revocation or query for a fresh one.
Blacklist-based revocation methods [14, 81] suffer from
expensive non-membership proofs. A possible optimization
is requiring the authority to maintain a sorted signature list of
revoked identifiers, enabling users to prove that their identifier
lies between two adjacent signatures [19, 67]. Yet, construct-
ing, updating, and distributing such lists remains costly. One
way to reduce this update overhead is by restricting updates
to specific time intervals/epochs; however, this sacrifices the
timeliness of the revocation. In [16, 74], users can present
a limited number of unlinkable non-revocation tokens per
epoch without expensive proofs or communication with the
RA. However, their approach requires an expensive genera-
tion of a blacklist of all revoked tokens for the RA and high
costs of non-inclusion checks for verifiers. Furthermore, un-
linkability does not hold if the verifier colludes with the RA.
Distributing the RA is a prominent approach to avoid single
points of failure [45,50,53]. Our scheme relies on adistributed
RA under an anytrust assumption, where the user’s privacy is
guaranteed if at least one out of nRA s does not collude with
the adversary. Clients do not need to know whichRA is not
compromised. We compare our scheme with the recent multi-
party revocation scheme ALLOSAUR [53], which is based
on accumulators under an anytrust assumption. ALLOSAUR
is not designed for deployments at continental scale, and we
show in §7.2 that we improve revocation cost by a factor of
20,000×for 10M credentials and a 1% revocation rate.
Private information retrieval. PIR allows a client to retrieve
an element from a database without revealing the queried
index to the server. PIR schemes fall into two categories:
multi-server PIR, which relies on multiple non-colluding
servers [25,27,33,35,47,66,69], and single-server PIR, which
uses asymmetric cryptography [31, 52, 59, 63, 69]. In multi-
server PIR approaches, the introduction of schemes based on
distributed point functions and function secret sharing has en-
abled sublinear communication [11, 12, 43, 57]. Recent years
have seen significant progress in single-server PIR, largely
due to advances in homomorphic encryption. Early works
such as XPIR [62] and SealPIR [2] demonstrated query com-
pression over RLWE (e.g., [13]), achieving sublinear com-
munication. Later schemes, including SimplePIR [52] and
FrodoPIR [31], eliminated the ring structure and achieved
throughput comparable to multi-server PIR by operating di-
rectly on LWE [73], but required clients to download and
store large initializationclient-hints. Later works [51, 59, 65]
removed this requirement at the cost of higher per-query
overhead. Another line of research [15, 41, 64], combines
RLWE with GSW [42] to represent queries in high dimen-
sions, achieving polylogarithmic communication at the ex-
pense of heavy computation and large per-client evaluation
keys (orserver-hints).
Despite these advances, many of the PIR techniques are
incompatible with our setting. Real-time credential revocation
involves frequent database updates, making approaches with
expensive, data-dependent client hints impractical, as their
costs cannot be amortized across many queries. Similarly,
server-side hints such as per-client evaluation keys are infea-
sible, since reusing the same key would break the anonymity
required for anonymous credentials.
While PIR and PRC share similar settings and construc-
tion mechanisms, they have orthogonal goals. In §5.2, we
highlight these differences and further provide a naive revo-
cation approach based on PIR. We evaluate this approach

showing that beyond protecting database privacy, our con-

Committed OT/authenticated MPC.Committed oblivious
transfer [28,29,40] is an OT protocol, in which the participants
commit to their inputs. Unfortunately, committed OT incurs a
cost linear in the number of records. Similarly, authenticated
MPC [34] provides a way for participants to authenticate their
inputs to an MPC protocol, but these schemes still require
communication linear in the input size.
Blind signatures.Similar to PRC, blind signatures [24, 72]
let client obtain signatures on a message, without revealing it
to the signer. In contrast, in our revocation andPRC setting,
the signer must attest to the validity of a data record and not
on a freely chosen message. This enforces a specific structure:
clients only choose a record index, not its associated content.
Self-sovereign identity.Self-sovereign identity (SSI) is an
architectural model for user-centric identity systems allowing
users to control and present their credentials without relying
on central identity providers [56]. SSI is commonly instan-
tiated using decentralized identifiers and verifiable creden-
tials [61, 77–79]. Anonymous credentials can be viewed as
verifiable credentials that additionally provide anonymity and
unlinkability, and SSI systems can be instantiated with them.
Revocation mechanisms for non-anonymous credentials, such
as the W3C Bitstring Status List [60], rely on credential iden-
tifiers as part of the revocation status check, which is incom-
patible with anonymous credentials where no such identifier
can be disclosed to preserve anonymity. We focus on revo-
cation for anonymous credentials; instantiating SSI systems
with them is outside the scope of this work.

4 Anonymous Credential Revocation System
This section introduces the system model, requirements, and
definitions for anonymous credential revocation.
4.1 System Model
In an anonymous credential revocation system, a client holds
a credential and proves its non-revocation status to a veri-
fier. The client first obtains a non-revocation token for its
credential from a distributed RA and then presents the token
to the verifier together with the credential. For privacy, theRA
should not learn for which credential the token was requested.
Conceptually, this approach is similar to the online certifi-
cate status protocol (OCSP) with stapling, where web servers
obtain a signed OCSP response from an OCSP responder to
prove their certificates’ validity to a client. In our setting, prior
to presenting a credential, a client obtains a non-revocation
token from the RA to prove its validity.
The system model (Figure 1) consists of four types of
parties: 1) theissuer, which issues anonymous credentials
to clients; 2) theclient, which holds a credential and wants
to present it while proving that it has not been revoked; 3)
theverifier, which checks the credential’s validity, including
non-revocation, before providing a service or granting access;
and 4) the RAs, which jointly maintain a revocation-status
database indicating whether each credential is valid or re-

voked. We consider distributed RAs: n authorities provide
the service jointly, and clients or verifiers may interact with
them to determine or prove a credential’s validity. How the
current state of the database is replicated across allRAs is out
of scope of our work.
Threat model.We consider adversaries that compromise par-
ties either semi-honestly or maliciously. Asemi-honestcor-
ruption reveals the party’s internal state, private values, and
view, including all incoming and outgoing messages, but does
not allow deviations from the protocol. Amaliciouscorrup-
tion additionally allows the adversary to deviate from the
protocol and to drop or modify the party’s messages. For
the distributed RAs, we use an any-trust assumption: the ad-
versary may compromise at most n−1 RA s, but clients or
verifiersdo notneed to know which RA is honest. We assume
communication channels provide confidentiality and integrity
(e.g., via TLS), and we do not consider side-channel attacks.
4.2 Requirements & Trust Assumptions
We state the functionality, privacy, and deployment require-
ments for anonymous credential revocation systems. Next, we
explain our trust assumptions for these properties. For formal
definitions, we refer to the full version of the paper [36].
Functionality.
RQ.F1: Revocation.Clients can obtain a valid non-revocation
token for a credential iff the credential is not revoked, and
cannot obtain one for a revoked credential.
RQ.F2: Dynamic Database.The revocation-status database
can change at any time, and updates occur frequently.
RQ.F3: Real-time Revocation.Once a credential is re-
voked, the window in which the holder can still present a
non-revocation token must be minimal to prevent abuse. We
target validity windows of a few seconds, which is inherently
incompatible with settings where both the client and verifier
are offline and cannot reach the RAs.
Privacy.
RQ.P1: Client Unlinkability.Adversaries should not be able
to link multiple non-revocation presentations of a client.
RQ.P2: Revocation Unlinkability.The RAs should not learn
information about the client, including the client’s credential
validity, revealed attributes, or the verifier to whom the client
presents the non-revocation token.
RQ.P3: Database Privacy.The revocation-status database
held by the RAs should remain private.
Efficiency.
RQ.D1: Scalability.The system must support nationwide cer-
tificate revocation in Europe at a scale exceeding 100M users.
RQ.D2: Lightweight Clients.Clients may run on smartphones
with limited resources. Their computation and communication
costs must remain small.
RQ.D3: Single Query Efficiency.In on demand scenarios,
users typically present a single non-revocation token per inter-
action. Hence, revocation systems cannot amortize cost over
batched queries for one user, and must be efficient for single
non-revocation queries.
Trust assumptions.We trust issuers to issue only valid cre-
dentials. We trust RAs to revoke a credential iff it is no longer
valid, and not to help clients produce valid non-revocation
tokens for revoked credentials. In the same way that verifiers
trust issuers to honestly issue credentials, they trust RAs to
check whether credentials remain valid. We separate issuance
from revocation for clarity; in practice, issuers and RAs may
be operated by the same parties.
Different properties rely on different adversarial models:
AM.1: Client Unlinkability.The adversary may maliciously
compromise the issuer, the verifier, and up ton−1 RA s, and
try to link different interactions of the same user.
AM.2: Revocation Unlinkability.The adversary may mali-
ciously compromise the issuer and up ton−1 RA s, and try to
learn attributes, the credential’s validity, or to which verifier
the client presents the credential.
AM.3: Forging Non-Revocation Tokens.The adversary may
maliciously compromise a client and semi-honestly compro-
mise up to n−1 RA s, and try to obtain a valid non-revocation
token for a revoked credential. Thus, compromised RAs may
leak their internal state and views, but do not actively deviate
from the protocol to help the client to forge.
Existing non-private revocation systems typically rely on a
single RA that is trusted for revocation. Mechanisms such as
certificate transparency [58] can provide accountability, but
they substantially increase system complexity, and adapting
them to a private setting such as ours is non-trivial and costly.
We acknowledge that our distributed RA design increases the
active-compromise attack surface: unlike a single-RA design,
an adversary that actively compromises one of thenRA s may
affect revocation correctness.
AM.4: Database Privacy.The adversary may maliciously
compromise multiple clients and try to learn information
about the revocation-status of non-compromised clients.

Limitations.Our model relies on an any-trust assumption for
the RAs: if all RAs are compromised, they can break client
privacy. Moreover, we do not guarantee unforgeability against
actively malicious RAs, and actively corrupting a single RA
is enough for forging non-revocation tokens, which increases
the attack surface. These limitations are not inherent to revo-
cation, but existing solutions that avoid this trust assumption
incur orders-of-magnitude higher costs. Our design makes a
scalability/trust tradeoff: it supports billions of credentials at
the cost of stronger trust assumptions. We note that distribut-
ing the RAs does not reduce availability: as long as at least
two of the nRA s remain reachable, clients can still obtain
non-revocation tokens, and correctness and unforgeability are
unaffected by the reduced number of available RAs.

5.2 Our PRC Construction at a High Level
We provide a high-level overview of our PRC protocol, but
first, we study a naive solution to discuss how our problem
differs from PIR and how this impacts the challenges we face.
At first glance,PRC resembles PIR, where a client retrieves
a record rec= D[idx] without revealing the index idx to the
servers. However, there is a fundamental difference: InPIR,
the client learns an unknown value rec, whereas in PRC, the
client already knows rec and seeks a certificate attesting a
statement about a record held at a private indexD[idx] to third
parties. For example, downloading the entire database satis-
fies thePIR functionality, but offers no benefit forPRC, where
the client aims to prove a statement about the database value
at a private index. For our revocation use case, we addition-
ally want certificates to admit efficient proofs-of-knowledge,
avoiding the need to revealidxandrecfor verification.

to clients retrieving the corresponding signature on a commit-

5.5 Security of ourPRCConstruction
We present proof sketches for ourPRCconstruction here, and
defer formal proofs to the full version of the paper [36].
Informal Theorem 5.1(Unforgeability).Our PRC con-
struction achieves unforgeability if the threshold signa-
ture scheme TSig is unforgeable, and the commitment
schemeComis binding.
For unforgeability, we consider a malicious client and up
to n−1 semi-honest PRC servers. Since all PRC servers
follow the protocol, the client can only obtain signatures on
commitments to an index and its corresponding record value
and auxiliary data. A certificate forgery thus either requires
opening a valid certificate to different content (commitment
binding) or forging a signature.
Informal Theorem 5.2(Unlinkability).Our PRC construc-
tion achieves unlinkability if the commitment scheme Com is
hiding and the correlated randomness is private.
We require the correlated randomness to be private,
i.e., randomness shares of honest parties are uniform and
unknown to the adversary. Our security argument is similar to
the approach of Araki et. al. [3] for malicious privacy. Given
private correlated randomness, we obtain protection against
malicious PRC servers even though we use a semi-honest
MPC protocol [44]. An adversary may tamper with shares,
breaking integrity and introducing additive errors; however,
since no output value is reconstructed, these errors remain hid-
den and leak no information. In more detail, our construction

Security analysis.Next, we provide an intuition why our
construction fulfills the security properties defined in §4.3.
Other credential properties of interest, such as device-binding,
are inherited by the underlying credential scheme and out
of scope of our discussion. Our construction links a PRC
non-revocation token to a credential presentation with a zero-
knowledge proof, ensuring that both components use the same
index idx without revealing it. Given the completeness and
knowledge-soundness of our proof system, revocation correct-
ness and unforgeability follow directly from the properties
of PRC and the underlying credential scheme. In addition,
database privacy trivially follows fromPRC. Revocation un-
linkability follows from the unlinkability of the PRC scheme,
since the adversary only observes the non-revocation token
retrieval and not the credential presentation, which an honest
verifier obtains. Client unlinkability requires the adversary
to choose challenge credentials that have the same revealed
attributes and revocation status, leading to the following two
cases: 1) If both credentials are revoked, the presentations
abort after retrieving an invalid non-revocation token, and un-
linkability follows from PRC. 2) If both credentials are valid,
the client retrieves a non-revocation token with a fixed status
rec=1 and presents it alongside their credential. While the
adversary knows the one-to-one mapping between retrieval
and presentation sessions, unlinkability follows from combin-
ing the unlinkability of the PRC and the credential scheme.
Instantiation.We can efficiently instantiate our PRC scheme
with a simple signature scheme like Schnorr [76], or thresh-
old variants like [9, 26, 55], and the Pedersen commitment
scheme [70]. The anonymous credential scheme may be in-
stantiated with BBS/BBS+ [17, 80], or PS [71] credentials.
These schemes support efficient zero-knowledge proofs , prov-
ing the credential and commitment both contain the same
index, via a standardΣ-proof of discrete logarithm equality.

7.2 Performance
We evaluate the performance of ourPRC and revocation sys-
tem, comparing it with the baseline and existing solutions.
Latency.Our system is designed to achieve low latency, and
our MPC computation only requires six rounds of interac-
tions. Most of this round complexity arises from the Boolean-
sharing to one-hot-encoding conversion (Alg. 3), which has
depth lglg(N) and requires four rounds for databases with
N=1 billion records. The retrieval step (Alg. 4) requires a
single round to compute the second inner product. We further
adopt a depth-optimized, single-round, Boolean to arithmetic
conversion based on [30] using DaBit [75]. We evaluate the
latency of our PRC from receiving a query to generating the
token σrec. The left plot in Figure 3 shows system latency as
the number of PRC servers varies, with RTTs ranging from
20 ms to 80 ms. Our solution achievesreal-timeresponses
with latency below 400 ms. The plot also indicates that la-
tency is dominated by the network delay rather than the num-
ber of credentials, servers, or computation cost. We observe a
step increase on all curves after 216 records, consistent with
our theoretical analysis, as BIN2OHE requires an additional
round once the index size exceeds 28 bits.

8 Conclusion
We have presented an anonymous credential revocation sys-
tem that satisfies all requirements defined in §4.2. We have
shown that our scheme achieves the revocation functionality
(RQ.F1) and the privacy properties in §5.5. We now demon-
strate that the remaining requirements are also met.
Functionality.In our system, credentials can be dynamically
revoked with a single bit-flip, with no overhead for clients
or verifiers (RQ.F2). We achieve latency below400 ms with
precise timestamps and full privacy, eliminating long token
validity periods and enabling real-time revocation (RQ.F3).
Efficiency.Our system scales to N=1 billion credentials
with under 25 ms of computation per RA, far exceeding the
requirement to support 100 million users (RQ.D1). As we
do not amortize costs, our system also satisfies single-query
efficiency (RQ.D3). Requesting a non-revocation token re-
quires negligible computation and 144 B of transfer from
clients in a two-server setting, overshadowed by TLS over-
head. Token presentation is comparable to non-anonymous re-
vocation, adding a minimal overhead of verifying a signature
and a discrete-log equality proof, thus supporting lightweight
clients and verifiers (RQ.D2).

B Extensions of our AC Revocation System
We discuss three extensions of our anonymous credential re-
vocation system in §6: enabling offline clients to delegate
contacting RAs to the verifier, achieving multi-show presen-
tations of non-revocation tokens, and database privacy.
Extension with offline clients.Real-time revocation (see
RQ.F3) requires connection to the RAs. However, as long as
the verifier can reach the RAs, our construction can support
offline clients that perform a one-round presentation with a
non-revocation check and no privacy degradation. This im-
proves support for constrained clients without internet access,
such as scenarios with a phone without mobile data.
Observe that in a usual presentation with a non-revocation
check, a client 1) performs a PRC request to obtain a non-
revocation token and 2) generates a zero-knowledge proof
linking the token to its credential. In this proof, the client only
needs to know the commitment value corec inside the token,
but does not need to prove over the token (i.e., signature)
itself, which is revealed to the verifier in the clear. Since
the commitment randomness is chosen by the client, and
it can assume that its credential is valid (i.e.,D [idx] =1 ),
the client can pre-compute the commitment without actually
receiving the non-revocation token. Further, processing the
PRC response does not require any private client input and
can be fully revealed to the verifier.
In an offline setting, the client can thus prepare the PRC
request, encrypt each query under the public key of the cor-
responding RA, and delegate the interaction with the RAs to
the verifier. By also providing the zero-knowledge proof over
the pre-computed commitment, the verifier can check the pre-
sentation without a further round with the client. To prevent
replay attacks and in extension prevent a verifier from repeat-
edly checking the revocation status of a client, we encrypt
add a nonce to the queries. We further assume that the encryp-
tion scheme provides confidentiality and integrity, binding the
nonce to its query. If one of the RAs receives a nonce twice,
they can abort the protocol. Due to the integrity protection, no
verifier can change the nonce associated with a query. In the
following, we assume that each RA is equipped with a public
key encryption key pair (pki,sk i). The offline presentation
and verification proceed as follows:

F Comparison with ALLOSAUR
ALLOSAUR [53] is the most closely related accumulator-
based revocation scheme in an anytrust setting. They utilize
MPC to let clients retrieve an updated witness for an accumu-
lator, which accumulates all non-revoked credentials. Unlike
our scheme, where updating the revocation database requires
only a single bit-flip, each revocation in ALLOSAUR incurs
linear work in the total number of credentials for the servers.
Client witness update costs additionally scale with the number
of revocations since the last update. In Figure 5 we compare
the server computation time for varying numbers of creden-
tials and revocation rates, using a RAs consisting of 2 PRC
servers. Note that while ALLOSAUR costs depend on the
number of revocations, our scheme only depends on the num-
ber of credentials. At 1 billion credentials with a 1% revo-
cation rate, ALLOSAUR requires approximately 2663 s per
witness update, while our solution requires only 10.294 ms,
resulting in a speedup of over 20,000×. We provide more
details on the comparison in the full version [36].
