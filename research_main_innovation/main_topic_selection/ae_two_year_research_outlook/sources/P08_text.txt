Abstract—Account ecosystems comprise a set of resources,
including online accounts, devices, and credentials, that
jointly impact a user’s or organisation’s security. Prior work
shows that users frequently overlook subtle dependencies
within these ecosystems, leading to security breaches that
are difficult to detect using analysis techniques focused on
isolated components.
We introduce resource access graphs (RAGs), a for-
mal model for the automated analysis of dynamic account
ecosystems. RAGs extend previous work on account access
graphs (AAGs) with partially ordered labels for vertices and
edges, enabling both general and resource-specific actions
to be modelled. To capture the dynamic nature of account
ecosystems, we define a transition system based on graph
rewriting with RAGs as states in which both user actions and
adversarial capabilities are modelled as graph transforma-
tion rules. Security properties are specified in computation
tree logic (CTL) allowing analysis using existing graph-
rewriting based model checkers, such as GROOVE.
We demonstrate our approach on a simple Apple account
ecosystem with the recent security feature Stolen Device
Protection (SDP). Our analysis reveals limitations in SDP,
affecting both the integrity and availability of a user’s Apple
Account and we propose and verify an improvement to
enhance account integrity.
Index Terms—account access graphs, formal modelling, for-
mal verification, security properties, graph rewriting
1. Introduction
Our reliance on Internet services has led to an ecosys-
tem of interconnected online accounts, devices and cre-
dentials so large that it is infeasible for an individual, let
alone a large organisation, to understand all the subtle
dependencies in their account ecosystems and the impli-
cations of the ongoing changes to them. Standard methods
for identifying security vulnerabilities focus on individual
issues in isolation, such as finding weak passwords, eval-
uating a specific service provider’s security, or formally
verifying an authentication protocol. With these methods it
is very difficult to identify vulnerabilities caused by multi-
ple layers of interconnected access methods and exploited
by attackers, e.g. [1], [2].
This work was supported by the Engineering and Physical Sciences
Research Council [grant number EP/W524463/1].
Account access graphs (AAGs) were introduced to
model and analyse the interconnections between accounts,
devices and credentials [3] and used to identify vulnera-
bilities in real users’ account ecosystems [4], [5]. This
vulnerability analysis is static in that it does not consider
that an account ecosystem changes over time. Account
access graphs with state [6] were subsequently proposed to
analyse the security of account ecosystems by modelling
user and attacker actions. They were used to manually
analyse an attack pattern on iPhone users reported in [2],
but cannot faithfully model complex account ecosystems.
We discuss these and other related works in Section 7.
In this paper, we build on the above work to introduce
the first formal model for the specification and automated
verification of dynamic account ecosystems. We model all
actors and items relevant to account security as vertices
and relations between them as edges in a resource access
graph (RAG) which represents a state of an account
ecosystem. We use graph rewriting to model intended
and adversarial actions in the system. User and adversary
actions are specified as a set of graph transformation
rules, encoded in our transformation graphs. The vertices
and edges in our RAGs have partially ordered labels
which allow us to model complex account ecosystems
where some actions apply in general while others apply
to specific types of resource only. We specify security
properties of an account ecosystem as computation tree
logic (CTL) formulas [7] where atomic propositions are
graph properties of the RAG.
Throughout this paper we use a simple Apple account
ecosystem as a running example to illustrate our approach.
We show that our formalism captures the account takeover
attack manually reconstructed in [6] and demonstrate auto-
matic discovery of the attack and verification of the recent
Stolen Device Protection (SDP) security feature with the
graph rewriting tool GROOVE 1 [8] .
Our contributions are as follows: We introduce RAGs,
transformation graphs, and property graphs to model ac-
count ecosystem state, graph transformations, and proper-
ties, respectively. These set the foundation for the auto-
mated analysis of account ecosystem security and enable
systematic analysis of a service provider’s account secu-
rity features and a user’s account security posture. Our
running example includes reusable patterns for modelling
account ecosystems and we provide modelling guidance

for user and adversary capabilities, reducing the overhead
of developing models in future work. We then use our for-

2.2. Graph Rewriting
The domain of graph transformations, also known as
graph rewriting, has been studied extensively for over 50
years. We refer to [9], [10] for a comprehensive intro-
duction and to [11] for a concise tutorial. Our definitions
in this paper are based on the so-called double pushout
(DPO) approach with injective matching [12].
We specify graph transformations by rules consisting
of two graphs L and R with a clean intersection. To
transform a graph G there must be a subgraph of G
isomorphic to L, i.e., there must be an injective graph
morphism m: L ↣ G. We call m a match and m(L) an
occurrence of L in G. The occurrence of L in G is the
part of G that will be transformed. Note that there may
be multiple occurrences of L in G giving rise to different
transformations depending on the chosen match.
The clean intersection of L and R represents the part
of the matched graph G which glues the changes and itself
remains unchanged by the transformation. Any elements
that are in m(L) but not in m(L⊓R) are removed from the
graph G. This yields an intermediate graph which is then
extended to a graph H – the result of the transformation
– by attaching new elements to m(L ⊓ R) corresponding
to the elements that are in R but not in L ⊓ R.
For the transformation to be applicable as described,
we must first ensure that the removal of vertices from G
does not leave any dangling edges behind. The so-called
dangling condition states that if a transformation deletes
a vertex from G then it must also specify the deletion of
all edges incident to that vertex.
Definition 4 (Dangling condition). Let L and R be
graphs with a clean intersection, G a graph and
m: L ↣ G an injective graph morphism. The dan-
gling condition is satisfied if no edge in EG \ m(EL)
is incident to any vertex in m(VL \ VR).
We additionally require rule-based conditions, so-
called negative application conditions [13], that guard the
application of a rule to a graph. To specify a negative
application condition, we include additional ‘forbidden

3. Resource Access Graphs
3.1. Definition
Account access graphs (AAGs) [3] model a gives-
access-to relation between resources in an account ecosys-
tem which are represented by vertices. Edge colours are
used to distinguish between different access methods. A
resource access graph (RAG), shown in Figure 4, extends
an AAG with partially ordered labels for edges and ver-
tices. The purpose of a RAG is to represent an account
ecosystem’s state, which in comparison to an AAG in-
cludes additional information about which resources an
agent, such as a user or adversary, has access to.
The addition of vertex and edge labels allows us
to model alternative access methods as specially-labelled
vertices, removing the need for an additional edge colour
mapping. Later we show how this makes it straightforward
to apply graph rewriting for labelled directed graphs as
described in Section 2. We first give the formal definition
of the RAG and explain and illustrate its distinguishing
components, the partially ordered vertex and edge labels,
afterwards.

3.2. Vertex Labels
Throughout this paper we make use of three incom-
parable top-level vertex labels: resource, agent, and
A. Vertices with label ⪯T resource represent resources
in the account ecosystem that have actions performed
on them by agents, represented by vertices with label
⪯T agent. Agents can gain access to resources via ac-
cess methods, which are represented by vertices with label
⪯T A. We now discuss each of these in more detail and
refer to Figure 3 for an illustration of the partial order on
labels used in the running example.
3.2.1. Resources. We distinguish between three more
specific types of resource: account, credential,
and device. Additionally, the labels pin, password,
auth_token, and biometric are more specific than
credential and model separate types of credentials,
while apple is more specific than account and models
an Apple Account.
3.2.2. Agents. Vertices with label ⪯T agent represent
entities that can modify the system. In this paper we differ-
entiate between legitimate users of the system, represented
by vertices labelled user, who only utilise intended
system behaviours, and attackers, labelled adversary,
who may perform malicious actions.
3.2.3. Access Methods. The third type of vertex in our
RAGs are access methods. A vertex with label An ⪯T A
represents an access method where exactly n resources
(the sources of incoming edges) are required to gain access
to the target resource of the outgoing edge. A vertex with
label An thus has exactly n incoming edges and exactly
one outgoing edge. Alternative access methods for the
same resource are modelled as separate vertices. Note that
for the running example only single-factor and two-factor
access methods are required, and hence only A1, A2 ≺T A
are included in the set of vertex labels.
3.3. Edge Labels
Edge labels model different access relations between
vertices. In this paper we limit ourselves to two basic
and incomparable access relations: the ‘gives-access-to’
relation (label ga) and the ‘has-access-to’ relation (la-
bel ha). Both of these edge labels can be refined by
distinguishing additional characteristics such as recovery
methods, physical containment, privilege levels or time
limits.
A ha edge from a vertex with label ⪯T agent to
a vertex with label ⪯T resource indicates that the
agent has access to the resource. Edges between vertices
⪯T resource and vertices ⪯T A are labelled ga. If a
vertex ⪯T A has n incoming ga edges and one outgoing
ga edge, then access to the source vertex of all incoming
edges is required to gain access to the target vertex of the
outgoing edge.

4. Transformation Graphs
4.1. Definition
A transformation graph is a RAG with an additional
mapping λ that assigns transformation labels to vertices
and edges. It specifies a rewrite rule for RAGs, where
the transformation labels indicate the conditions for the
transformation to be applied and which elements are added
or removed. Transformation graphs thus allow us to update
a RAG to reflect changes made to the modelled account
ecosystem.

[PDF PAGE 7]
Definition 9 (Transformation graph). A transformation
graph T is a tuple T = (G, λ), where G is a RAG
and λ: VG ∪ EG → L Λ is a mapping from ver-
tices and edges to the transformation labels LΛ =
{add, remove, require} ∪ {forbidi}i∈N.
A transformation graph corresponds to a graph rewrite
rule (L, R, N) (Definition 6) where the left-hand graph L
contains all elements with transformation label require or
remove, the right-hand graph R contains all elements with
label require or add, and N is the set of graphs ˆLi, each
containing all the elements with label require, remove, or
forbidi, for i ∈ N.
To apply a transformation graph T to a RAG G, the
elements of T with label require and remove are matched
with a corresponding subgraph in G such that the match
cannot be extended to any of the forbidden subgraphs
given by the require, remove, and forbidi labels. Next,
elements corresponding to those with label remove are
removed from G. Finally, new elements corresponding to
those with label add are added to G. A match is only valid
if the label of each matched element in the RAG is equal
to or more specific than the label of the corresponding
transformation graph element.

5. A RAG Transition System
A RAG transition system consists of a set of RAGs
which are the states of the system and transformation
graphs which define the transition relation and also serve
as transition labels. We use this system to formally model
how an account ecosystem changes over time and to
analyse its security properties.
Definition 15 (RAG transition system). A RAG transition
system is a labelled transition system (σ0, Σ, ∆, ⇝)
where
1) Σ is the set of states, i.e. the set of all possible
RAGs,
2) σ0 ∈ Σ is the RAG giving the initial system state;
3) ∆ is the set of transition labels, i.e. the set of
transformation graphs for the system;
4) ⇝⊆ (Σ×∆×Σ) is the transition relation defined
by (σ, δ, σ′) ∈⇝ if and only if σ′ ∈ δ(σ), i.e. the
RAG σ′ is an element of the set produced by the
application of the transformation graph δ to σ.
The evolution of an account ecosystem is influenced
by three types of entities:
• Vendors: These are the account, software, and
hardware providers that offer and modify services
and products with specific features and access re-
strictions. They thus define the intended behaviour
of the account ecosystem.
• Users: The users build their account ecosystem by
adding, using and discarding specific services and
products offered by the vendors. They are defined
by the user model.
• Adversaries: The adversaries interact with and
may compromise the services and products in the
user’s account ecosystem. They are defined by the
threat model.
To analyse the security of an account ecosystem for
a given set of vendors, a particular user, and a type of
adversary, we must specify the transformation graphs that
model the account ecosystem’s intended behaviour, user
model and threat model. We discuss these in the remainder
of this section after introducing the fixed access rules that
every account ecosystem is subject to.

5.2. Intended Behaviour
The intended behaviour of a system arises from the set
of actions that are made available by the vendors through
their services and products. These are actions any agent of
the system can take, such as creating an account, logging
in to an account, changing an account password, adding
a recovery method or setting up a new device.
A vendor’s services are modelled by specifying a set of
transformation graphs. They generally introduce a vendor-
specific label to ensure the modelled actions apply to the
vendor’s accounts and devices only and use a generic
agent vertex so that the actions can be performed by
any agent, including an adversary. Our model therefore
captures attacks which exploit ‘intended’ system features
to enter an insecure state.

5.3. User Model
An account ecosystem’s user model uses labels more
specific than user to represent different user profiles.
This is useful in order to distinguish between different
users in a multi-user account ecosystem or to model
any particular user’s skills and fallibility. We only briefly
sketch the latter.
To compare the security of a system’s intended be-
haviours under different user models, several more specific
user labels are defined and the user vertex in the initial
state is replaced by the appropriate more specific label.
For example, a regular user can be defined as capable of
performing all intended actions of the system, while an
unskilled user is only capable of a subset of these and a
skilled user may have access to additional actions, such
as monitoring for and removing an adversary’s access to
particular types of resources. Orthogonally, a fallible user
can inadvertently perform harmful actions, such as losing
access to a device or credential.
5.4. Threat Model
An account ecosystem’s threat model is defined by
the adversary capabilities given by the adversary rules.
These model malicious behaviours which an adversary
may use as part of an attack. Previous research on ac-
count access graphs [3] has observed that there is no
single threat model that applies to all users. Depending
on the threats relevant to a specific user, different attacker
capabilities are added to the set of transformation graphs.
For example, a very strong adversary may compromise
any resource at any time. This is modelled by the com-
promise
resource transformation graph in Figure 14 in
which a strong_adversary that is more specific than
adversary gains access to any resource.
Through careful design of transformation graphs and
the partial ordering on labels we can thus accommodate
various different threat models within a single transition
system. It is easy to see how this can be further refined to
model an adversary who can perform very specific attacks
only applicable to certain types of accounts or devices.
By only modifying which label is assigned to adversary
vertices in the initial RAG we can assess the security
properties of a system under various threat models.

6. Security Properties of Account Ecosystems
6.1. Property Graphs
We introduce property graphs, which use a restriction
of the transformation label mapping λ of transformation
graphs to specify properties of RAGs. A property graph is
a transformation graph that only uses the transformation
labels
require and forbidi (for i ∈ N). In other words, a
property graph only specifies a matching condition and
not a transformation on a RAG.
Definition 16 (Property graph). Let LP = {
require} ∪
{forbidi}i∈N be the set of property labels. A property
graph P is a transformation graph P = (G, λ) where
G is a RAG and λ: VG ∪EG → L P is a mapping from
vertices and edges to property labels. We say that P
is well-formed if the following substructures of P are
RAGs:
P↓{require},
P↓{require,forbidi} for all i ∈ N.
A property graph is applied to a RAG in the same
way as a transformation graph – a subgraph matching all
elements with label require must exist in the RAG, which
cannot be extended to a match for any of the forbidden
conditions. Note that for a well-formed property graph P
the graphs L and R in ρ(P ) will always be identical, since
no elements are added or removed. We say that a RAG G
satisfies a well-formed property graph P , denoted G |= P ,
if P can be applied to G. Formally, G |= P ⇔ P (G) ̸= ∅.

6.2. Computation Tree Logic
We use computation tree logic (CTL) [7] formu-
las to specify and verify security properties of account
ecosystems. The CTL formulas are expressed over atomic
propositions which are defined by RAG property graphs.
We consider two standard security problems in account
ecosystems: unauthorised access to resources and loss of
access to resources. To specify the security requirements
to prevent these two problems, we define (a parametrised
set of) atomic propositions and CTL properties for each
of them.
The first atomic proposition, which we call resource
compromise, is that an adversary has access to a resource.
The following definition states that a RAG satisfies the
(undesirable) resource compromise property if any agent
with label ⪯T adversary has access to any resource
with a given label.
Definition 17 (Resource compromise). A RAG G satis-
fies compromise(r) if it satisfies the property graph
(shown in Figure 15) where a vertex with label
⪯T adversary has access to a vertex with label
r ⪯T resource.
Resource compromise is a property of individual
states, or RAGs, in an account ecosystem. The related
(desirable) security property for a RAG transition system
is the CTL path property that an adversary can never
gain access to a resource with a given label. In other
words, there is no reachable state that satisfies resource
compromise. We call this property resource integrity.
Definition 18 (Resource integrity). A RAG transi-
tion system (σ0, Σ, ∆, ⇝) satisfies integrity(r) if
compromise(r) does not hold in the initial state σ0
nor in any state reachable from σ0:
σ0 |= AG ¬compromise(r).
We now turn to the second common security problem:
loss of access to a resource. The (desirable) security
requirement for an individual RAG is that a legitimate user
has access to a resource. We call this property resource
access.
Definition 19 (Resource access). A RAG G satisfies
access(r) if it satisfies the property graph where a
vertex with label ⪯T user has access to a vertex with
label r ⪯T resource (see Figure 15).
The related, desirable property of a RAG transition
system is the CTL path property that a user can eventually
gain access to a resource with a given label. We call this
property resource availability. We say that an account
ecosystem satisfies resource availability if it is always
possible for a user to gain access to a resource with a
given label.
Definition 20 (Resource availability). A RAG transition
system (σ0, Σ, ∆, ⇝) satisfies availability(r) if it is
always possible to reach a state that satisfies access(r):
σ0 |= AG EF access(r).
6.3. Account Takeover Attack
We now analyse the security properties of the sys-
tem defined in our running example. To achieve this we
provide an implementation of our model in the graph
transformation model checking tool GROOVE 3 [8]. The
final transformation graphs and initial state of the model
are given in Appendix A.
In our running example, we are interested in the
integrity and availability of the Apple Account. In other
words, the adversary should never be able to gain access
to the Apple Account and the user should always be
able to regain access to the Apple Account. The spe-
cific properties we consider are integrity(apple) and
availability(apple). The properties make use of the prop-
erty graphs compromise(apple) and access(apple).
Both properties are violated.
Integrity is violated because, after learning the iPhone
passcode and stealing the iPhone to reach state G2 (Fig-
ure 10), the adversary can perform the following steps to
compromise the Apple Account:
1) The adversary unlocks the iPhone using the
passcode (modelled by an application of gain
2
where the locked iPhone and passcode vertices
give access to the iPhone vertex).
2) The adversary gains access to the Apple Account
through the open session on the iPhone (modelled
by an application of gain
1 where the iPhone
vertex gives access to the Apple Account vertex).
3) The adversary now has access to the Apple Ac-
count vertex, so the compromise(apple) prop-
erty graph is satisfied and thus integrity(apple)
does not hold.
Continuing from the previous attack steps, the adver-
sary can now remove the MacBook as a trusted device
by entering the iPhone passcode. This is modelled by
an application of remove
device, where the adversary has
access to a trusted device (the iPhone) and its credential
(the passcode) and access from a second trusted device
(the MacBook) is removed.
From this state, there is no way for the user to regain
access to the Apple Account: they still have access to the
account password, but without access to the iPhone they

6.4. Stolen Device Protection
As a response to the account takeover attack outlined
in Section 6.3, Apple introduced Stolen Device Protection
(SDP) in iOS 17.3 [16]. When enabled, this optional se-
curity feature requires a user to authenticate with Face ID
instead of the device passcode/password before perform-
ing security-critical actions such as removing a trusted
device or changing their account password.
We can easily update our model to reflect a system
where SDP has been enabled. We do this by replacing the
user_choice vertex in the remove
device transforma-
tion graph (Figure 13) with a biometric vertex. The
transformation graph can now only be applied if an agent
has access to the Face ID vertex (with label biometric)
rather than the passcode vertex (with label pin). The full
updated model is given in Appendix A. Similar changes
for the extended example are given in Appendix B.
The results of the security analysis are that availability
of the Apple Account now holds, while integrity is still
violated. Integrity of the Apple Account does not hold
even with SDP enabled because SDP does not prevent the
adversary from unlocking the iPhone using the passcode
and accessing the open Apple Account session, including
sensitive iCloud data such as photos and files. Since the
adversary has no capability to compromise the Face ID
vertex, it is clear they can no longer remove the MacBook
as the trusted device, thereby preventing the attack on
Apple Account availability described previously. After the
iPhone is stolen, the user can still gain access to their
Apple Account from the MacBook. Our observation that
SDP is insufficient to prevent an attacker gaining access
to sensitive account data is a subtle pitfall that may not
be obvious to iOS users.
6.5. Proposed Improvement
To prevent the adversary from gaining access to the
Apple Account, we propose additionally requiring bio-
metric authentication for mobile devices, and password
authentication for laptop and desktop computers, every
time the Apple Account is accessed directly from a trusted
device. That is, rather than single-factor access from a
trusted device to the Apple Account, two-factor authenti-
cation using the trusted device and its biometric credential
should be required. This ensures that integrity of the
Apple Account holds, thereby preventing an adversary
from viewing private account data such as the user’s email
address and list of trusted devices.

Limitations of the Analysis. The availability property
holds in the setting of this model, but not in general. In this
model we allow the adversary to apply the steal
device
capability only once. Clearly, if the adversary stole all
of the user’s trusted devices (both the iPhone and the
MacBook in this example) the user would then have no
method to access their Apple Account. Moreover, our user
model considers an infallible user. A fallible user could
misplace their trusted devices.
If we remove the steal_counter vertex from the
steal
device transformation graph (thus allowing the ad-
versary to steal an unlimited number of devices), availabil-
ity no longer holds. We believe this assumption is realistic
for many users, who may only have a single Apple device
(for example, an iPhone). It is also unlikely that such a
user would have an additional phone number independent
from their iPhone to use as a trusted phone number (an
alternative to a trusted device). These users are at risk
of permanently losing access to their Apple Account if
their device is stolen, even when SDP is enabled. This is

[PDF PAGE 14]
another subtle pitfall which may not obvious to iOS users.
Our model only includes the minimal entities and
transformation graphs required to demonstrate the features
of our formalism, yet it still raises interesting questions
about the security properties of such systems, captures
existing attacks and helps to assess the success of recent
security features in mitigating them. In particular, with our
running example we have both demonstrated that SDP
is insufficient to ensure the integrity of a user’s Apple
Account and verified our proposed solution.
Readers interested in a more comprehensive system
model should refer to the extended example documented
in Appendix B, which includes additional system features
(modelled as transformation graphs) which do not affect
the outcome of our security analysis.
7. Related Work
7.1. Account Access Graphs
Account access graphs (AAGs) were introduced by
Hammann et al. [3] as a formal model of the dependencies
between a user’s accounts, devices, and credentials. Ham-
mann et al. showed how these dependencies can be used
to identify vulnerabilities in users’ account ecosystems by
choosing a scoring scheme and assigning security scores
to the vertices in the AAG. The security analysis is based
on the weakest set of credentials sufficient for access
to an account and the most unreliable set of credentials
necessary to maintain access to an account. This approach
thus relies on the analyst’s experience and awareness of
attacks when assessing the strength of credentials. More-
over, while the approach considers which credentials an
attacker could gain access to, it does not consider the
actions an attacker could carry out to modify the user’s
account ecosystem. For example, in the attack reported in
[1], the attacker exploited a flaw that allowed anyone to
add a new credit card number to a victim’s account. This
new credit card number could then be used as a credential
to gain access to the victim’s account through account
recovery. We include a GROOVE model of this attack
using our formalism along with our other supplementary
example files.
Our work considers how an access graph changes as a
consequence of both user and adversary actions and allows
for more general security properties to be analysed. It can
also be seen as complementing Hammann et al.’s work in
that the results of our security analysis could be used both
to inform the assignment of security scores to vertices in
an AAG and explicitly show the attack steps that exploit
identified vulnerabilities.
Arnaboldi et al. [6] have built upon Hammann et al.’s
work to create ‘AAGs with state’. In this model a set
of agents is associated with each vertex to model which
agents have access to which vertices. Our access model is
more general in that any vertex with access to another
vertex is modelled with an appropriate edge label. In
particular, we model agents as vertices. This generalises
and simplifies the formalism and allows all updates to the
system to be expressed as graph transformations. Graph
transformations as applied in this paper are a more natural
choice for the transition system because they directly
operate on subgraphs which naturally correspond to an
account ecosystem’s access methods and complex access
relationships.
Despite the addition of state, Arnaboldi et al.’s model
still has limitations when it comes to accurately repre-
senting real-world attacks. Their transition system consists
of updates to individual elements of the graph, such as
removing or adding a single edge or vertex. For example,
the creation of a new account can only be modelled by
a sequence of multiple transitions where the intermedi-
ate AAG states are incorrect representations of the real
world. Arnaboldi et al. attempt to mitigate these issues
using tactics, a concept borrowed from interactive theorem
proving, to combine a series of transitions into a single
action or attack pattern. Their approach can thus represent
any known attack by letting the tactic be the attack steps.
They cannot usefully discover attacks, because their low-
level operations allow for spurious attacks arising from
unrealistic behaviour. Their tactic language can describe
sequences of operations to try, but tactics are not well-
suited to preventing operations that are valid in the transi-
tion system. With the additional limitation that their tactics
have no variables, a separate, complex tactic is needed for
every graph analysed. Replacing Arnaboldi et al.’s low-
level operations with our graph transformation-based rules
would fix the spurious attacks.
7.2. Interconnected Accounts
The problem of authentication vulnerabilities arising
from interconnected accounts was independently explored
in [18]. Further work on vulnerabilities in interconnected
systems provides a more general model of relationships
between cyber, physical, and human entities [19]. How-
ever, the model is only capable of static analysis and
does not capture changes which may be made to the
system over time. Our model, in contrast, allows for the
analysis of dynamic and temporal security properties. Our
partially ordered labels allow various abstract entities and
relationships to be modelled within the context of a RAG.
This flexibility means our model is not limited to systems
involving online accounts and could also be applied to
other areas within the cyber-physical domain.
7.3. User studies
AAGs as defined by Hammann et al. [3] and a variant
by P ¨ohn et al. [20], [21] have been successfully utilised
in user studies [4], [5], [22]. Both lines of work provide
tools to extract relevant information from users and au-
tomatically generate their AAGs [5], [22]. B ¨uttner and
Gruschka [22] used AAGs to analyse the authentication
and recovery methods used with both Google and Apple
accounts and concluded that many users are dependent on
a single trusted device to maintain access to their account.
Our state-based approach to modelling an Apple Account
ecosystem allows us to consider how authentication meth-
ods may change over time, which is an area identified
by B ¨uttner and Gruschka as future work. We also expand
upon B¨uttner and Gruschka’s model by considering Stolen
Device Protection and show that it is insufficient to ensure
availability for users with a single device.

8. Conclusion and Future Work
8.1. Conclusion
We introduced resource access graphs (RAGs) and
transformation graphs as a formal model for analysing
dynamic account ecosystems that evolve through both
legitimate user actions and adversarial behaviour. We
modelled the dynamic behaviour of a user’s account
ecosystems as a labelled transition system, where the
state of the user’s account ecosystem is a RAG and the
set of possible user and adversary actions is a set of
transformation graphs. To reason about the security of
account ecosystems we formalised account integrity and
account availability properties as CTL formulas evaluated
over the transition system’s state space.
We validated our approach on several models of an
Apple account ecosystem with and without the recent
security feature Stolen Device Protection (SDP) enabled.
The analysis automatically rediscovered a known account
takeover attack and identified subtle weaknesses in the
security guarantees provided by SDP. In particular, we
showed that while SDP can restore account availability
after device theft, it does not prevent integrity violations
by a thief in possession of a user’s trusted device and
passcode. We proposed and verified an improvement over
SDP and discussed the limitations of our proposal with
respect to account availability.
Overall, this work demonstrates the ability of our
formalism to capture real-world attacks and assess the
impact of changes to a system’s behaviour on its under-
lying security properties. Our work thus sets the formal
foundation for the automated analysis of the security of
people’s and organisations’ account security posture.
8.2. Future Work
The correspondence between our transformation
graphs and graph rewriting allows our future work to
draw from the extensive existing body of work on graph
rewriting (e.g. [10], [12], [23]–[25]). It also allows us
to utilise the existing graph rewriting tool GROOVE [8]
to work with our formalism while developing a more
efficient verification approach and a dedicated verification
tool. In the following, we give a few examples.
8.2.1. Decidability. Plump [25] has shown that termi-
nation of graph rewriting with the double-pushout ap-
proach is undecidable in general. More recently, K ¨onig
and Str ¨uckrath [24] applied the theory of well-structured
transition systems (WSTSs) to graph transformations to
determine settings in which graph transformation systems
are decidable. It is thus easy to give artificial examples
of transformation graphs with undecidable properties, but
it is an open question whether K ¨onig and St ¨uckrath’s
approach can be adapted to our case. Specifically, our
need for transformation graphs with forbidden edges and
vertices prevents a straightforward adaptation.
8.2.2. Scalability. The transition system used as a running
example in this paper consists of 21 vertices in the initial
state graph and 5 transformation graphs. This generates
a state space of 692 states for the model without SDP
enabled, and 522 states with SDP enabled. GROOVE
can compute the entire state space in under 2 seconds.
However, scalability becomes an issue when increasing
the number of vertices or transformation graphs, which
is necessary to more accurately reflect the behaviour of
a large system. For example, our extended example has
16422 states when SDP is disabled, taking approximately
10 minutes to explore. While existing tools such as
GROOVE provide some mechanisms (e.g., rule priori-
tisation) for reducing the state space, the feasibility of
analysing large graph rewriting systems is a known issue
[23]. It is therefore necessary to investigate methods for
improving the efficiency of analysing security properties
of large models.
8.2.3. Improved Modelling of Real-World Account
Ecosystems. The flexible nature of our partial orders
on labels for vertices and edges in RAGs allows for a
variety of systems and threat models to be represented.
Although our model is dynamic, the transition system
uses a fixed set of transformation graphs representing
the actions agents can take within the system. In real
systems, vendors modify the actions users can take both
to enable additional functionality and to patch security
vulnerabilities. Permitting the set of transformation graphs
to change would more accurately reflect the evolving
nature of account ecosystems. This would allow us to
model vendor agents capable of modifying the system
behaviour and thus to explore attacks in the malicious
vendor threat model or to offer practical advice to vendors
to ensure their changes do not introduce vulnerabilities
into users’ account ecosystems.
8.2.4. Empowering Users. In this work, we have lim-
ited ourselves to modelling the two standard properties
associated with account ecosystems, viz. integrity and
availability. Our approach is sufficiently general to model
entirely different properties that may be of interest to real
world users such as privacy, access control or resilience.
As well as verifying the security properties of existing
systems, we would like to offer actionable steps to users
or vendors to improve their account ecosystem, e.g., after
an incident. This requires the implementation of a bespoke
user-friendly tool.
