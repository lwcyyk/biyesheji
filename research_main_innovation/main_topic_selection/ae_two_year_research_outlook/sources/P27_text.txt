Controlling Patient Data Access

Abstract—Healthcare data breaches cost hospitals and clinics
an average of $7.42M USD per breach. Autonomous multi-agent
LLM pipelines in clinical settings multiply that exposure struc-
turally: a prompt-injection payload embedded in a laboratory
report or referral note traverses the full delegation hierarchy
before any human reviewer observes it. Standard mechanisms
including ﬂat Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) and Macaroon
caveat chains provide no structural guarantee that a spawned
sub-agent’s permissions remain bounded by its parent’s scope.
The Minimum Necessary Standard must hold across mixed-
sensitivity EHR data and multi-hop chains simultaneously. That
is, the gap is architectural, not behavioral. We present the Hy-
brid Scripted-Execution and Prompt-Grounded Injection (SEPI)
design, a replay-faithful methodology that freezes authorization-
request traces and replays them identically across baselines to
isolate authorization semantics from LLM stochasticity. Applied
to a ﬁve-agent post-surgical oncology coordination workﬂow over
a synthetic Electronic Health Record scenario, and evaluated
against 39 clinically grounded injection payloads across leaf-
agent, mid-delegate, and parent-orchestrator compromise sce-
narios, NGAC-hypergraph time-scoped delegation reduces mean
sensitivity-weighted blast radius ( Bw) by 75.0% relative to
ﬂat RBAC and 65.2% relative to Macaroon caveat chains. A
temporal ablation shows that removing texpire raises mean Bw
by 75.0% relative to full NGAC. The reduction follows from
structural properties of the delegation architecture: attenuation
is provable by construction rather than contingent on model
behavior . Across model conditions, Claude Sonnet 4.6 actively
detected all 39 payloads while open-source models showed weaker
or intermittent behavioral protection, reinforcing authorization
as the model-agnostic containment control.
Index Terms —Agentic AI, prompt injection, NGAC-
hypergraph, blast radius, clinical AI security, HIPAA, access
control, multi-agent systems
I. I NTRODUCTION
IBM’s 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report [1], data
breaches at hospitals and clinics cost an average of USD 7.42
million ($7.42M USD) per breach. That ﬁgure predates the
widespread clinical deployment of LLM agents. Integration of
such agents into Electronic Health Record (EHR) workﬂows
introduces a new exposure class, not merely a wider one.
Traditional software vulnerabilities are bounded by the process
perimeter of a single application. Multi-agent orchestration
frameworks such as AutoGen [2] dissolve that perimeter.
Delegation chains now carry context across agent boundaries
automatically, without human inspection at each traversal hop.
An adversarial payload embedded in a retrieved laboratory
result or a cross-facility referral note can instruct a sub-agent
to request resources outside its intended scope. That instruction
travels the full delegation hierarchy before any human reviewer
observes it.
The healthcare setting ampliﬁes this risk along three dimen-
sions. First, EHRs contain data spanning multiple HIPAA sen-
sitivity tiers in a single patient record, from routine structured
lab values to psychiatric and substance-use histories. Second,
the Minimum Necessary Standard (45 C.F.R. § 164.502(b))
[3], [4] requires that access be scoped to the minimum
PHI necessary for the stated purpose. Standard RBAC role
assignments and stateless token caveats cannot structurally
enforce this principle across multi-hop delegation chains.
Third, documented oncology-sector breaches [5] and sector-
wide breach cost data [1] establish that the consequences of
unauthorized PHI access are severe and sector-speciﬁc.
A. Five-Agent Oncology Workﬂow
The ﬁve-agent post-surgical oncology care-coordination
pipeline described in Figure 1 centers on a Task Planner
(orchestrator) that receives the shift context and spawns four
sub-agents: the EHR Retriever, the Clinical Calculator, the
Pharmacovigilance Monitor, and the Synthesis/Output Gener-
ator. Each agent communicates exclusively through a tool-call
interface. All database access is mediated by the enforcement
point.
A controlled study of 216 patient-facing clinical dialogue
simulations found aggregate prompt-injection success rates of
94.4% at the primary decision turn across frontier models [6],
with 91.7% success even for attacks involving teratogenic
drugs. These ﬁgures establish that, for models deployed
without strong safety alignment, the authorization layer is
the primary, not secondary, control against injection-induced
unauthorized access.

II. R ELA TED WORK
A. Multi-Agent LLM Orchestration and Agentic Attack Sur-
faces
Multi-agent orchestration frameworks such as AutoGen [2]
expand the attack surface from a single context window to
every node in the delegation hierarchy: a message in one
agent’s context window can traverse the entire delegation
hierarchy before human review is possible. The Model Context
Protocol [7] standardizes tool attachment for agents but simul-
taneously standardizes cross-origin context-poisoning vectors,
while an EHR-integrated clinical deployment reported that
PHI-touching interactions account for over 70% of physician-
initiated sessions [8]. A recent scoping review likewise ﬁnds
that healthcare-agent governance frameworks lag deployment
practice [9].
B. Prompt Injection Benchmarks and Clinical Risk
Lee et al. report a 94.4% aggregate injection success rate
across 216 clinical dialogue simulations. In the highest-harm
scenario category, i.e. cases involving teratogenic drugs and
other FDA Category X agents, injection success reached
91.7% [6]. That ﬁgure represents the conditional success
probability given that a payload reaches the model’s context.
Tool-integrated agents face a different distribution: InjecAgent
reports 24–47% indirect injection success for ReAct-prompted
GPT-4 agents [10], while Agent Security Bench reports
72.68% for direct injection and 84.30% for mixed attacks [11].
The Medical Prompt Injection Benchmark [12] demonstrates
that harmful LLM outputs can be structurally coherent and
clinically decorated while recommending dangerous injected
actions. W ASP reports partial hijacking rates up to 86% while
full attacker goal completion remains 0–17% [13], motivating
our separation of compromisedand exﬁltration states.
C. Delegation and Access Control for Agent Workﬂows
RBAC [14] provides no structural attenuation for spawned
agents. Macaroons [15] provide cryptographic caveat chain-
ing. Stateful extensions [16] address revocation but cannot
detect semantically valid yet unauthorized intent. NGAC [17]
evaluates authorization via graph traversal and enforces pol-
icy at the resource layer independently of the application.
A NIST instantiation demonstrates NGAC trapping SQL
queries [18], directly analogous to our tool-call interception.
Translating NGAC to a hypergraph formulation makes sub-
agent permission sets mathematically bounded by the parent
hyperedge: attenuation is provable by construction rather than
by conﬁguration. A prior hypergraph privilege-analysis study
demonstrated the scalability of this approach across multi-
cloud environments accommodating large-scale principal and
object sets [19]. The present work applies that formulation to
the distinct problem of temporal, multi-hop LLM delegation,
for which the time-scope attribute of the hyperedge is not
merely convenient but structurally necessary.
D. Clinical AI Governance and Quantitative Risk Modeling
HIPAA audit controls (45 C.F.R. § 164.312(b)) [20] and
HHS OCR audit ﬁndings [21] establish that systematic ePHI
governance gaps are the norm, not the exception. A recent ar-
chitectural analysis [22] identiﬁes three structural tensions be-
tween LLM context windows and HIPAA minimum-necessary
requirements, all of which are addressed structurally by the
hypergraph delegation layer. Digital forensics research [23]
establishes that semantic drift across multi-hop chains can
break forensic causation chains even with complete logs. No
prior work models multi-agent LLM blast radius using an

III. S YSTEM MODEL AND THREA TSCOPE
A. Threat Model and Authorization Scope
Figure 1 illustrates the NGAC-Hypergraph blast-radius con-
tainment architecture. We consider an adversary capable of
embedding adversarial payloads in external content that the
clinical agent system ingests during normal operations, in-
cluding laboratory report free-text ﬁelds, referral notes, and
medication-history entries retrieved from simulated external
systems. This is an indirect prompt injectionthreat consistent
with MITRE A TLAS techniques [24] and the scenarios studied
by Lee et al. [6] and InjecAgent [10]. The adversary’s objective
is to cause one or more sub-agents to request PHI beyond the
current delegation scope, either by crossing patient boundaries
or by escalating to a higher HIPAA sensitivity tier than the
task requires; the adversary does not control model weights,
system prompts, or the authorization policy store.
Our focus is the authorization threat surface after context
poisoning occurs: how much unauthorized access does the
enforcement architecture permit before blocking? We there-
fore exclude direct compromise of the NGAC policy store,
inference-time model-weight manipulation, side-channel text
exﬁltration, and cross-organization federated EHR settings,
and we do not claim generalization beyond the synthetic
oncology workﬂow and payload corpus used in Section IV.
B. NGAC-Hypergraph Authorization Layer
Next Generation Access Control (NGAC) [17] represents
access policy as a directed graph over user nodes (U), object
attributes (OA), policy classes (PC), and operations. We extend
this to a hypergraph formulation in which each delegation edge
is a time-scoped hyperedge: the child agent’s permission set is
a proper subset of the parent’s set, and the hyperedge carries
explicittstart andtexpire attributes.
Deﬁnition 1 (Time-Scoped Delegation Hyperedge). A dele-
gation hyperedgee =( P,C,ts,te,O) connects parent agent
P to child agent C, is valid over the interval [ts,te], and
authorizes only the operations in O,w h e r eO⊆O P (i.e.,
the child’s authorized operations are a strict subset of the
parent’s).
Attenuation is provable by construction: graph traversal can-
not reach any node reachable fromP that is not also reachable
through e. A spawned sub-agent cannot hold permissions
outside this hyperedge regardless of what an injected payload
instructs it to request.

IV . EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN (SEPI)
A. Design Principle: Generate-Replay Separation
The SEPI (Hybrid Scripted-Execution + Prompt-Grounded
Injection) methodology enforces one non-negotiable separa-
tion: generation may be stochastic; replay must be determin-
istic.
All baseline comparisons operate on frozen, serialized
TraceRecordobjects. No LLM call is made during the
replay or measurement phase. This design follows the method-
ology of AgentDojo [25] (formal environment-state utility
checks, not LLM simulation) and AgentSpec [?] (runtime rule
enforcement over permitted agent control ﬂow). The unit of
analysis is the frozen trace, not the LLM output.
B. Frozen Trace Generation
We generate N =5 0 synthetic oncology patients using
Synthea [26] (cancer module, seed = 20260101). The patient
bundle is exported to a ﬁxed SQLite database whose SHA-256
hash is recorded in everyTraceRecordheader, proving that
all primary trace ﬁles operate on identical data. The scripted
workﬂow executor in oncology_workflow.pytraverses
the ﬁve-agent task graph in topological order for a given
patient ID and shift start time and emits a ParsedAction
containing agent identity, target resource identiﬁers, requested
operation, delegation-edge context, and timestamp relative to
shift start. No LLM calls are made; given the same patient ID
and scenario ID, the output event stream is bit-for-bit identical
across runs.

The four baselines are RBAC, Macaroons, NGAC-hypergraph

produced 36 replay traces plus 9 base traces (45 JSONL ﬁles

VI. D ISCUSSION AND THREA TS TO VALIDITY
A. Structural vs. Empirical Claims
Our primary claim in this work is structural: NGAC-
hypergraph delegation makes permission attenuation provable
by construction. The empiricalBw measurements validate that
this structural property produces measurably lower unautho-
rized access under our experimental conditions.
B. The Conﬁguration-Dependence Objection
NGAC-Hypergraph’s advantage is a function of the policy
conﬁguration as well as the architecture. The supplemen-
tary materials include the complete NGAC-Hypergraph policy
Y AML ﬁles to allow independent replication. The formal
property being claimed (child permission set is a strict subset
of parent permission set under time-scoped hyperedge) holds
for the workﬂow as conﬁgured. Delegation overbreadth (the
fraction of edges where this property holds) is zero under
NGAC-hypergraph, whereas RBAC exhibits nonzero over-
breadth in the primary experiments, providing a metric-level
conﬁrmation.
C. Threats to V alidity
Prompt sensitivity. Injection payloads were manually
crafted for the oncology context. We mitigate prompt sensitiv-
ity concerns using two surface-form variants per payload. We
averagedBw across variants.
Model stochasticity. Live validation runs use tempera-
ture =0 . Claude Sonnet 4.6 results (195 runs, 0 out-of-
scope tool calls, 39/39 explicit injection detections) are treated
as a conﬁrmed bound: 95% CI upper bound on true out-
of-scope rate ≈ 1.5% (Clopper-Pearson exact, n = 195 ,
k =0 ). This threat applies to the Ollama model conditions,
where temperature=0 and model version are pinned. We do,
however, report variance across replicates.
Baseline conﬁguration fairness.All three baselines enforce
at the same architectural position (tool-call layer). RBAC
is conﬁgured to reﬂect documented healthcare deployment
practices [14]. Macaroons include standard time-bound caveats
but are not exhaustively conﬁgured.
VII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
We presented SEPI, a replay-faithful methodology for
comparing access-control architectures in multi-agent clini-
cal LLM workﬂows that decouples stochastic LLM behavior
from deterministic authorization measurement. Applied to a
ﬁve-agent oncology coordination workﬂow with a corpus of
39 clinically-grounded injection payloads, we demonstrated
that NGAC-hypergraph time-scoped delegation reduces mean
weighted blast radius by 75.0% relative to ﬂat RBAC and
65.2% relative to Macaroon caveat chains. A temporal ablation
showed that removingtexpire from the token raises mean Bw
from 1.78 to 3.11, a 75.0% increase relative to full time-
bound NGAC-Hypergraph. The result is therefore structural as
well as empirical: because child permissions are bounded by a
time-scoped hyperedge, attenuation is provable by construction
rather than left to prompt behavior or role conﬁguration. Live
validation across three model conﬁgurations showed hetero-
geneous model-level responses: Claude Sonnet 4.6 actively
detected all 39 payloads and issued zero exact target out-
of-scope calls, Llama-3.1 8B mostly persisted with in-scope
workﬂow behavior while still producing one off-target cross-
patient request, and Mistral 7B v0.3 exhibited intermittent
exact-match failures without any payload reaching the corpus-
validation threshold. The enforcement layer therefore carries
the containment burden regardless of model behavior. The
released artifact package includes the 39-payload corpus, all 45
JSONL traces, NGAC policy Y AML ﬁles, and the validation
harness needed to reproduce the reported measurements.
Future work has four priorities:
1) Replace expert-elicited CTMC ampliﬁcation priors with
the empirical containment ratios reported here.
2) Quantify authorization latency across local-library,
localhost-HTTP , and networked deployment modes in-
cluding remote-VPC deployment modes.
3) Develop policy-speciﬁcation tooling that reduces
NGAC-Hypergraph conﬁguration overhead.
4) Characterize the transition point at which frontier-model
behavioral detection and formal authorization shift from
complementary to partially redundant controls, partic-
ularly as ﬁne-tuning and domain adapters alter model
behavior in on-premises clinical deployments.
