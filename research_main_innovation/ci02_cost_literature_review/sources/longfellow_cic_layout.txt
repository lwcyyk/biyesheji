                        1   Introduction

                        Chaum [Cha85] introduced the idea of ananonymous credential system, orpseudo-nym
                        systemasamethodtoprovideindividualsfullcontroloverthedisseminationofpersonal
                        information. Suchasystemconsists ofusers,issuers,and relyingparties. Issuersgivea
                        set of digital credentials to a user; users can prove to a relying party that they possess
                        credentials satisfying a property without revealing anything more than the fact that they
                        hold credentials that satisfy the property. Colluding relying parties are unable to link
                        a specific user to a pair of interactions they participate in. Even a colluding issuer and
                        relying party should not be able to link a specific user to a session.

ImpedimentsWhiletherehavebeenattemptstodeploycredentialsystemsfordigital
identity applications, such efforts encounter three major roadblocks.
    First, many identity issuers have deployed hardware security modules that only
supportstandardizedRSA or ECDSA signatures. Such organizations may be unwilling
or incapable of updating their infrastructure to support credential systems that rely on
newer cryptographic assumptions such as pairing-friendly curves or lattices.
    Asecondproblemisthatcredentialsareissuedbymultipleorganizations(e.g.,state
DMVs), and relying parties need to support all of them. The task of producing asingle
commontrusted parameterviaa securemulti-partycomputationceremony involvingall
issuersseemshighlyimpractical. Thissuggeststhateventheproposedanonymouscre-
dentialschemesdiscussedabovethatarebasedonstandardizedcryptographyareunsuitable
becausetheirproofsystemsrequire bilinear pairingsandlargetrustedsetupparameters.
    Another criticalimpediment is thepotential forscaled abuseofanonymous credentials.
If credentials can be copied from one malicious user to another, then the system may
not be able to provide any guaranteed soundness.  Because an anonymous credential
does not leak any information other than the shared attributes during a presentation flow,
it is more difficult to discover and/or revoke copied credentials just by observing the
presentationflows. Thisproblemariseseveninthesettingofcertificatesystemsthatonly
offer selective disclosure properties.
    Toaddressthiscopiedcredentialproblem,issuersofdigitalidentitycredentialsrequire
users to hold their credential using trusted hardware.  Modern mobile devices often
contain asecure elementwhich is a hardware module designed to be tamper-resistant,
side-channelresistant,andthussecureagainstmostkeyextractionattacks. Theidentity
protocols in the ISO MDOC standard, for example, specify issuance and presentation
flows that require the secure element on the receiving device to store a portion of the
credential. Most deployed secure elements only support legacy standardized signature
schemes such as RSA and ECDSA, and in particular, they do not support performing
operations on pairing-friendly curves,lattices, or even well-deployed signature schemes
such as EdDSA. It requires a decade to upgrade these elements in the field, which makes
the adoption of schemes such as BBS+ impractical.

Key ResultsOne obstacle to proving knowledge of an ECDSA signature is common
to elliptic curves that have been standardized. Standardized curves for ECDSA, such
as the NIST P256 curve, specify finite fields that do not have the roots of unity needed
for efficient NTT algorithms.  Consequently, prior workemulatesthe arithmetic of the
P256 field inanotherfinite field thatis optimized for ZKproofs. Evenusing very clever
arithmetic tricks, this overhead amounts to 40–100x increase in arithmetic operations.
    We overcome this bottleneck using several techniques. First, we design an NTT in
aquadraticfield extensionwhichallowsus toperformNTTs (infieldsthatdo nothave
proper roots of unity) at only≈ 1.5x the cost of doing so in an NTT-friendly prime of the
same size.
    We also develop a novel Reed-Solomon encoding approach which can exploit the
subfieldstructureofthefield,yieldingaconstant-factorimprovementinproofsizeover
the standard NTT-based methods.  Instead of defining the Reed-Solomon code as a
polynomialinterpolationovertherootsofunity,wedefinethecodeasaninterpolation
overanarithmeticsequenceofevaluationpointsinthebasefield. Wepresentamethodto
performtheinterpolationoverthesepointsusingalinearconvolution. Thus,whenour
witness values are over a small subfield or base field, even though the NTT itself may be
carriedoutoveralargerextensionfield,theresultingcodewordremainsinthesubfield,
yielding a smaller proof.
    Some applications require zero-knowledge arguments about the pre-image of cer-
tainnaturallyboolean functions such as                      SHA256. Following the work of Diamond and
Posen [DP25, DP24], we confirm the speedup possible with using the extension field
GF(     2k) to perform the sumcheck. In this case, the standard NTT algorithms no longer
apply, and we instead use the family of additive FFTs [LCH14]. Specifically, we develop
a newbidirectional truncatedadditive FFT to solve the problem of polynomial interpola-
tion over cosets. As our benchmarks show, this operation provides almost an order of
magnitude speedup in basic algebraic operations.
    Our next contribution stems from the specific circuits we developed for verifying
ECDSAsignatures,verifyingthepre-imageofSHA-256,andforparsingnesteddocument
formats such as CBOR that are specified in current identity credential standards such as
ISO MDOC. These components are necessary in order to satisfy our goal of constructing
proof of posession forlegacycredential formats.
    We alsopresent amethod to improveperformance whena ZK credentialverification
taskinvolvesbothECDSAsignaturesandSHA-256hashes. Priorproofsystemsverifya
singlearithmeticcircuitdefinedoveronefinitefieldF. However,asourbenchmarksshow,
itissubstantiallyfastertoverifypropertiesaboutSHA-256preimagesusingabinaryfield

6.1   A simple anonymous credential

Belenkiy etal. [BCKL08] introduce the notion ofa P-signatureas a component thatyields
aconstruction ofananonymous credentialscheme. Briefly, aP-signature schemeletsa
user (1) obtain a signature on a committed message without revealing the message, and
(2) construct a non-interactive zero-knowledge proof of knowledge of(F(msg)       ,σ)   such
that VerifySig(pk               ,msg,σ) =   1.  In the formal security notion, Belenkiy et al. [  BCKL08,
Definition 3] write, “Note that we require that only the user’s input msg is hidden from
the issuer, but not necessarily the user’s outputσ. The reason that this is sufficient is
thatin actualapplications (forexample, in anonymouscredentials), auser wouldnever
show σ in the clear; instead, he would just prove that he knows σ." In this way, the
formaldefinitionisweakerthanthatofblindsignaturesthataresometimesrequiredfor
anonymouscredentials. Itsufficesfortheusertoproduceapublickey(e.g.,forECDSA,
as this is a commitment to the corresponding secret key for this scheme), and incorporate
the public keyas a component in thesignature. In this way, this step is already subsumed
in many modern identity protocols which require device binding. It is also clear that
property (2), sometimes abbreviated as theproof of posession, for the P-signature is the
difficultpropertytoachieve inpriorwork. This proof of possessionisthesame primitive
that we construct above in §4.1.
    In analogy to [BCKL08, Theorem 1], we claim

Theorem 11(Informal). If ECDSA is a secure signature scheme, and Protocol2.5is a zero-
knowledge argument scheme, then there exists a secure P-signature scheme for ECDSA.

    Finally, applying Lemmas 2 and 3 from [BCKL08] imply the existence of a formal
anonymous credential scheme from ECDSA (and implicitly SHA-256).
    We reportdetails to thepractioner on howto instantiate theproof of posessionrequired
by a P-signature construction for a few concrete schemes.

A   The MDOC standard

MDOCs are specified in ISO 18013-5.  Here we summarize the relevant portion that
concerns a binary string which can be parsed into a hierarchical MSO object of the form:

{"version":  "1.0",  "digestAlgorithm":  "SHA-256",
"docType":  "org.iso.18013.5.1.mDL",
"valueDigests":  {
    "org.iso.18013.5.1":
         {13:  h’B628...69D2’,
         14:  h’8CFE...604C’,
       ...
    "org.iso.18013.5.1.aamva":  {
         15:  h’1034...402A’,
         ...
         8:  h’7AC6...8485’}
    },
"deviceKeyInfo":  {
         "deviceKey":  {1:  2,  -1:  1,
               -2:  h’7B8F...F321’,
               -3:  h’859E...772C’}
    },
"validityInfo":  {
         "signed":  0("2023-10-11T13:18:15Z"),
         "validFrom":  0("2023-10-11T13:18:15Z"),
         "validUntil":  0("2023-11-10T13:18:15Z")
    }
}

    "digestID":  14,
    "random":  h’8c082af3d5d78b98bcc00bd26fae5130’,
