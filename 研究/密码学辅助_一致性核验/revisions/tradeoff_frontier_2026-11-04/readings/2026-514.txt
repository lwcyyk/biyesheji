Secure Multi-party Matrix Invertibility Testing
over Small Finite Fields
Seungwoo Han , Jooyoung Lee , Seungmin Park and Mincheol Son
KAIST, Daejeon, South Korea
Abstract.Multi-party matrix invertibility testing over finite fields of small order or
characteristic is a pivotal operation for thresholdizing multivariate quadratic (MQ)
signature schemes. However, achieving perfect privacy in a constant number of
rounds remains a challenge: existing solutions either suffer from information leakage,
failing to provide perfect privacy, or require high computational and communication
overhead, in particular, whenp≤n , where p and n denote the characteristic of the
underlying field and the matrix size, respectively.
To address this limitation, we propose two protocols for multi-party testing of matrix
invertibility. The first protocol extends the Cramer-Damgård protocol to fields of
small order by employing the field lifting technique. The second protocol is based on
multi-party computation of the Samuelson-Berkowitz algorithm, specifically designed
for fields of small characteristic. Both protocols are formalized in the arithmetic
black-box (ABB) model with Shamir’s secret sharing scheme.
We show that both protocols achieve perfect privacy while allowing for a tradeoff
between input-independent offline rounds and input-dependent online rounds, where
the input corresponds to the shared matrices. Specifically, the first protocol runs in7
offline rounds with communication complexityO(N n4)and in3online rounds with
communication complexity O(n4), and the second protocol runs in3offline rounds
with communication complexityO(n4)and in9online rounds with communication
complexityO(n 4), wherenis the matrix size andNis the number of parties.
Keywords:Multi-party Computation·Linear Algebra·Matrix Invertibility Testing
1 Introduction
Secure execution of linear algebra algorithms in the multi-party setting is a cornerstone of
privacy-preservingcomputationwithapplicationsincludingzero-knowledgeproofsviaMPC-
in-the-Head, private set intersection (PSI), privacy-preserving machine learning (PPML),
and threshold multivariate quadratic (MQ) signatures. For instance, secure linear regression
may require solving a secret-shared normal-equation system or inverting a secret-shared
matrix during training. In particular, matrix invertibility testing over finite fields of
small order or characteristic serves as a fundamental building block for thresholdizing
MQ signature schemes [CEN25, ABCN26], where the underlying MQ schemes include
UOV [BCD+24] and MAYO [BCC+23].
In general, an MPC protocol is designed to achieve perfect privacy, ensuring that no
private information is leaked to an adversary during the execution of the protocol. However,
while several constant-round threshold MQ signature protocols have been proposed [CS19,

1.1 Related Work
In the plain setting, matrix invertibility testing is typically performed using Gaussian
elimination. However, its straightforward application to the multi-party setting poses
significant challenges due to its high round complexity and conditional pivoting operations.
Instead of employing this sequential algorithm, various constant-round protocols have been
proposed [BIB89, CD01, CKP07, MW08, MV24]. In this section, we briefly review these
existing protocols and highlight the limitations that our work aims to address.
Bar-Ilan-Beaver Protocol.Bar-Ilan and Beaver [BIB89] proposed several constant-
round multi-party protocols for computing inverses in a group, multiplying multiple
matrices, and generating random invertible matrices. Given a group(G,·)and a share
of an elementg∈G, denoted[g], the basic idea of computing[g−1]is to mask[ g]with a
random share[r]and reveal[ r·g]. In this way, each party can locally compute(r·g)−1,
and then[g−1] = (r·g)−1·[r]. The idea of random masking can be extended to secure
matrix invertibility testing: given a matrixA∈Mn(Fq), each party generates a share of a
random invertible matrixR∈GLn(Fq). Then they jointly computeA·R, and hence its
determinant. This method is called the one-sided masking protocol. It has been used to
construct the first threshold MQ signature [CS19].
Two-sided masking Protocol.Bar-Ilan and Beaver [BIB89] proposed several constant-
round multi-party protocols for computing inverses in a group, multiplying multiple
matrices, and generating random invertible matrices. Given a group(G,·)and a share
of an elementg∈G, denoted[g], the basic idea of computing[g−1]is to mask[ g]with a
random share[r]and reveal[ r·g]. In this way, each party can locally compute(r·g)−1,
and then[g−1] = (r·g)−1·[r]. The idea of random masking can be extended to secure
matrix invertibility testing: given a matrixA∈Mn(Fq), each party generates a share of a
random invertible matrixR∈GLn(Fq). Then they jointly computeA·R, and hence its
determinant. This method is called the one-sided masking protocol. It has been used to
construct the first threshold MQ signature [CS19].
Later, Celi, Escudero, and Niot [CEN25] showed that the one-sided masking protocol
potentially reveals the column space of the matrixA, increasing the guessing probability of
the oil space. To address this weakness, they proposed a two-sided masking protocol with
two random invertible matricesR,S∈GLn(Fq), where the parties jointly compute the
determinant ofR·A·S. The two-sided masking protocol prevents leakage ofA’s column
space, while it still reveals the rank ofA. In the context of threshold MQ signatures,
leaking the column space ofA may reveal information of the secret-key componentO. By
contrast, although rank leakage means that the protocol does not achieve perfect privacy,
there is no known concrete key-recovery attack that exploits only the rank ofA.
This two-sided masking protocol is also adopted in [ABCN26]. While their work
optimizes the number of rounds after the message is given in threshold MQ signing to
single-round, the matrix invertibility test itself is still instantiated with two-sided masking
protocol.
In [CEN25], the ideal functionality explicitly outputs the rank ofA as leakage. Our
protocols instead realize the standard ideal functionality for matrix invertibility testing,

Cramer-Damgård Protocol.Cramer and Damgård proposed a constant-round protocol
for several linear algebra tasks, including determinant computation, rank evaluation, and
solving linear systems over finite fields [CD01]. Their protocol for invertibility testing
exploits certain properties of the characteristic polynomial, providing strong privacy
guarantees.
Given a matrixA∈Mn(Fq), letχA(x)denote the characteristic polynomial of A,
namely,
χA(x) = det(xIn−A).
Since χA(x)is of degree at mostn, it is uniquely determined by its evaluations atn + 1
distinct field elements. Moreover, the determinant ofA corresponds to its constant term,
up to a sign:
χA(0) = det(−A) = (−1)n detA.
In this protocol,n + 1distinct public pointsz1,z 2,...,zn+1∈Fq are fixed. For any
z∈Fq, the matrixzIn−Ais invertible if and only ifz is not an eigenvalue ofA. SinceA
has at mostn eigenvalues, a uniformly randomz makeszIn−Ainvertible with probability
at least1−n/q. When q is sufficiently large, matricesMi =ziIn−A, i = 1,...,n + 1,
will be invertible with high probability.

is done by LU decomposition, where a lower-triangular matrixL and an upper-triangular
matrix U are sampled uniformly at random and the random matrix is defined as their
product, namelyR=L·U.

properties ofA. Finally, the distribution of the random maskR, being the product of
lower-triangular and upper-triangular matrices, is not uniform overGLn(Fq), which may
further compromise privacy.

1.2 Our contribution
In this paper, we propose two perfect-privacy protocols for multi-party testing of matrix
invertibility, each of which addresses a specific limitation of existing protocols. The first
protocol extends the Cramer-Damgård protocol [CD01] to fields of small order by employing
the field lifting technique. The second protocol is based on multi-party computation of the
Samuelson-Berkowitz algorithm [Ber84], which is especially effective for fields with a small
characteristic wherep≤n. Our protocols are formulated as standalone MPC subroutines
for testing the invertibility of a shared matrix, and can be instantiated in any application
requiring this functionality.
These protocols are described in thearithmetic black-box(ABB) model, and their round
and communication complexities are analyzed with Shamir’s secret sharing. Consequently,
each construction is executed in a constant number of rounds with a tradeoff between
(input-dependent) online and (input-independent) offline rounds. In practical executions,
the offline phase can often be performed during idle time or instantiated via a trusted-
dealer preprocessing model, in which correlated randomness is distributed before the input
becomes available. We note that the online/offline terminology used in threshold MQ
signatures usually distinguishes the phases before and after the message is given. With
respect to this convention, the invertibility-testing subroutine studied in this work is part of
the offline phase of threshold signing, since it can be executed before the message is known.
Our online/offline distinction is instead internal to the invertibility-testing functionality
and separates input-independent preprocessing from the input-dependent computation
on the shared matrixA. This distinction is still meaningful in threshold MQ signatures:
the offline phase of our subroutine is independent of the signing key and can therefore be
prepared without knowing the secret key, for example, by a trusted dealer. In contrast,
the online phase involves the secret-shared, key-dependent matrixA, and hence must be
carried out by the signers unless key-dependent information is revealed to a third party. We

This framework is specifically instantiated to facilitate the asymptotic analysis of com-

channels or verifiable consistent transfer mechanisms.

2.4 Basic Protocols with Shamir’s Secret Sharing
For concrete analysis of complexity, we instantiate the ABB model with Shamir’s secret
sharing in the semi-honest, honest-majority setting. In this section, we review basic

2.5 Perfect Privacy of MPC Protocol
The concept of “revealing no information” (perfect privacy) is formally defined using the
real/ideal world paradigm. Proving perfect privacy of a protocol requires that whatever
information a corrupted party gathers during the protocol could have been entirely
simulated by them, using only their own input and the final output.
In the real world, each partyPi, i = 1,...,N , runs the protocol on private inputxi
while interacting with the other parties. When a protocolπis executed to compute a
certain functionalityf on their private inputsx = (x1,...,xN), all the information obtained
byP i is defined as
Viewπ
i (x) = (xi,ri,m 1,...,mρ)
where ri is the internal random coin used byPi during the execution of the protocol,
and mj is the messages received from other parties at thej-th round. For a subset
I⊆{1,...,N}of corrupted parties, we define their joint view as
Viewπ
I (x) = (Viewπ
i (x))i∈I.
In the ideal world, a trusted third party (TTP) receives the inputsx1,...,xN, computes
f(x1,...,xN), and returns the output. A simulatorS is an algorithm that, given only
the inputs of the corrupted parties and the output, generates a fake joint view for the
corrupted set. Perfect privacy requires that this simulated view be identically distributed
to the real joint view of the corrupted parties.
Definition 1(Perfect Privacy).A protocol πprovides perfect privacy against up toT
corrupted parties if there exists a probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) algorithmS such
that
{S((xi)i∈I,f(x))}≡{Viewπ
I (x)}
for every input vectorx= (x1,...,xN)and every subsetI⊆{1,...,N}with|I|≤T.

5 Cramer-Damgård Protocol over Extension Fields
In this section, we present protocols with perfect privacy for secure matrix invertibility
testing over fields of small order by lifting the Cramer-Damgård protocol to extension
fields. Although the original work briefly notes this lifting idea [CD01], it does not provide
a concrete analysis. We therefore give a detailed treatment, including a proof of perfect
privacy as well as explicit bounds on round and communication complexity.
In the original Cramer-Damgård protocol, two limitations make it difficult to achieve
perfect privacy. First, the protocol generates a random invertible matrix[R]together with
[det(R)]via an LU-decomposition-based procedure and usesR to mask the target matrix.
However, the distribution of matrixR is not uniform inGLn(Fq). Second, the protocol
computes det(Mi), whereMi :=ziIn−Afor fixed and distinct pointsz1,...,zn+1∈Fq.
During this computation, the rank ofMi is made public, which leaks whetherzi is an
eigenvalue ofA.
To address the first limitation, we employ Algorithm 5 to generate([R], [det(R)−1]),
ensuring thatR is uniformly distributed overGLn(Fq). To address the second limitation,
we perform the computation in the extension fieldFqn+1. Let Alift denote the natural
embedding ofA intoMn(Fqn+1). We samplezi∈Fqn+1 such that its minimal polynomial
over Fq has degreen + 1. Then zi cannot be a root ofχAlift(X), since any eigenvalue
of Alift is algebraic over Fq of degree at mostn. Equivalently, for everyi, the matrix
Mi := ziIn−Alift is nonsingular overFqn+1. Consequently,n is always revealed as the
rank ofMi over the extension field without any leakage of information onA.

7 Conclusion
In this paper, we addressed the challenge of privately testing matrix invertibility over
fields with small order or characteristic in the multi-party setting. Operating under the
arithmetic black-box model, we proposed two protocols specifically tailored for such fields
that achieve perfect privacy in constant rounds.
While this work establishes a robust foundation, several avenues for further research
remain. Practically, integrating these protocols into concrete MPC frameworks such as
SPDZ or RSS is essential to assess their performance under realistic network constraints.
Theoretically, reducing extension-field overhead and refining preprocessing constants remain
promising directions for enhancing overall efficiency.
