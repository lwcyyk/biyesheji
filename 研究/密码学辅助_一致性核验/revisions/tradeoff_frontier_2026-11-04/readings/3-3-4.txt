Constant-Round Secure Distributed Decoding
and HQC Threshold Decryption
Pascal Giorgi 1 , Fabien Laguillaumie 1 , Lucas Ottow 1 and
Damien Vergnaud2
1 LIRMM, Univ. Montpellier, CNRS, Montpellier, France
2 Sorbonne Université, CNRS, LIP6, Paris, France
Abstract.Threshold public-key encryption schemes enable decryption only with
the participation of enough partial secret key holders. In this article, we propose the
first dedicated protocol for distributed decryption of HQC ciphertexts. This protocol
is perfectly correct and does not leak any information about the shared secret key.
This leads to the first threshold cryptosystem based on HQC. To this end, we present
protocols for securely decoding shared erroneous words of both Reed-Muller and
Reed-Solomon codes. Such decodings require to develop innovative techniques for
specific multiparty computations in fields of characteristic 2. For distributed Reed-
Muller decoding, we investigate two approaches : one relying on a majority computing
protocol and a second one relying on an exaustive search for closest codeword. For
distributed Reed-Solomon decoding, we propose a novel protocol for securely solving
Padé approximants over shared polynomials. Beyond their immediate application
to HQC, our results enable new techniques in secure distributed computation over
structured algebraic objects, and may find independent applications in advanced
cryptographic protocols.
Keywords:HQC·Threshold cryptography·Multi-party computation·Reed-
Solomon decoding·Reed-Muller decoding
1 Introduction
Thresholdpublickeyencryption(t-PKE)[ DF90,Des93]isavariantofpublickeyencryption
(PKE) in which a number ofN participants receive a partial secret key. It is required from
such a primitive that at least a threshold numberT of these participants collaborate in
order to decrypt a given ciphertext. The need for threshold primitives is quite substantial,
as highlighted by NIST’s first official call for threshold schemes1 that was published in
early 2026.
A number of t-PKE instantiations already exist in the literature. Many of these rely
on classical problems in pre-quantum cryptography such as discrete logarithm-based prob-
lems [FP01, LP01, SG02, GJKR07], RSA-based problems [DDFY94, GRJK00], composite
residuosity [FPS01, DJ01, HMR+19], or class group-based problems [CLT22, BCD+24].
As such, these constructions would be vulnerable to quantum computers. Lattice-based
cryptography has been used to build post-quantum schemes. In 2010, Bendlin and
Damgård [BD10] designed the first threshold lattice-based encryption scheme. Since then,
threshold lattice-based schemes with additional properties have been proposed, such as
adaptive (or CCA) security [DLN+21], or fully homomorphic encryption [BS23]. In order

Code-based threshold cryptography.In the realm of cryptography based on error-
correcting codes, threshold schemes are less numerous. Takahashi, Hashimoto and
Ogata [THO24] proposed the first three threshold PKEs based on code-related assumptions.
In order to design their schemes, the authors use the Niederreiter cryptosystem [Nie86],
which is used in the Classic McEliece NIST submission [ABC+22], as a baseline. Then, they
use different conversion techniques for each of their proposition in order to build threshold
schemes achieving CCA security. Their first two propositions use general conversions
that do not rely on the specific properties of the Niederreiter cryptosystem, and the sizes
of the elements of their schemes depend on the numberN of participants of the scheme.
Their last contribution follows a different design; the authors build a dedicated multiparty
computation (MPC) protocol for decoding Goppa codes, which is the main step in the
decryption procedure of the Niederreiter cryptosystem. Naively, building an MPC protocol
for decrypting a PKE ciphertext allows one to build a threshold scheme by first sharing
a secret key of the base cryptosystem. Similar ideas were also used to build threshold
multivariate-based digital signatures [CS19, CEN25]. Unfortunately, the MPC protocol
of [THO24] would fail when run on incorrectly formed ciphertexts. This prevents their
third scheme from achieving CCA security, even when using an additional conversion
aiming at achieving this level of security.
In [GLOV26], the authors fix this issue by designing a conversion based on the
Naor-Yung transform [NY90, Lin03], and by instantiating it using a custom-made Zero-
Knowledge proof system for Niederreiter ciphertexts. This construction prevents the MPC
protocol from being run on incorrect ciphertexts by requiring each party to publicly verify
that a submitted ciphertext is correctly constructed, and this leads to a CCA construction.
The authors of [GLOV26] also provide an additional improvement that allows reducing
the partial secret key size compared to the third but erroneous scheme of [THO24].
All of these schemes rely on the Niederreiter cryptosystem [Nie86], which suffers from
large public key sizes compared to other PKEs. It would therefore be preferable to also
dispose of threshold PKE schemes that rely on other code-based constructions. In 2025,
NIST selected the HQC cryptosystem [AAB+22] as a standard alternative to the lattice-
based primitives that were previously selected to be standardized. As in the Niederreiter
cryptosystem, the core of the HQC cryptosystem, called HQC.PKE, requires decoding an
erroneous codeword as the main part of the decryption procedure. Moreover, these codes
have symbols either overF2 or one of its extension fields. For the HQC.PKE cryptosystem,
this code is a concatenated code with some variants of first order Reed-Muller codes
and Reed-Solomon codes. However, main differences arise in the decoding procedures of
Niederreiter and HQC.PKE. Decoding during Niederreiter decryption is done on a private
Goppa code (defined by the secret key) and for which the number of errors is known in
advance. Decoding for HQC.PKE is done on a public code (part of the parameters of
the scheme), and only an upper bound on the number of errors is known. This calls for
distinct techniques when trying to come up with an MPC protocol for decoding these
codes, especially for the computation of the error polynomials in the case of either Goppa
codes or Reed-Solomon codes. In the plain setting, decoding Reed-Solomon codewords
usually relies on the Berlekamp-Massey algorithm or the extended Euclidean algorithm,
which are challenging to translate into constant-round MPC protocols.
Constant round MPC protocols for computer algebra problems.As decoding
error-correcting codes efficiently in the plain setting relies heavily on computer algebra
techniques, it is necessary to understand to what extent these techniques are applicable in
the MPC setting.

Multiparty computation as a field dates back to the seminal works of Yao [Yao82]
and Goldreich, Micali, and Widgerson [BGW88]. Since then, a number of constant-round
protocols for dedicated purposes have been published. The seminal work of Bar-Ilan and
Beaver [BIB89] presents techniques for general constant-round computation of general
arithmetic circuits. Since then, a multitude of works have been published in order to
improve this generic construction through more dedicated constant-round protocols.
In the realm of linear algebra, the work of Cramer and Damgård [CD01] was the first
to propose dedicated MPC protocols, but these protocols had some error probability that
made them not perfectly correct, i.e. the protocols could sometimes leak information about
the secret matrix. In the context of threshold decryption, such a leakage could help an
adversary learn information about the secret key. Improved protocols in terms of efficiency
were proposed in [CKP07] and [MW08], but still encompassing error probabilities. The
work of Maire and Vergnaud [MV24] introduced the first protocol for computing the
determinant of a shared matrix in a perfectly correct manner. However, their techniques
require the coefficients of the shared matrix to be in a field of large characteristic, which
does not correspond to the setting of decoding codewords over extension fields ofF2.
A very recent ePrint paper [HLPS26] actually provides a constant-round protocol for
computing determinant over a small characteristic field, but with a quite significant addi-
tional communication cost. In general, multi-party computations over fields of characteristic
2 are more challenging than over large prime fields.
Mohassel and Franklin [MF06] were the first to present dedicated protocols for un-
bounded shared polynomial multiplication, shared polynomial evaluation and interpolation
over shared points. These protocols were later improved in [GLOV24] to achieve a quasi-
linear communication complexity. While an MPC protocol for polynomial gcd has already
been presented in [MF06], it does leak some information on the input polynomial, which
is unacceptable in our setting. More advanced techniques with perfect correctness and
privacy are thus required to tackle those challenging polynomial calculations arising within
the decoding of some error-correcting codes. In particular, for Reed-Solomon decoding, one
needs to provide a dedicated MPC protocol for solving Padé approximant problems, i.e.,
find a rational fractionr
t =fmodX n given a polynomialf and some degree constraints
on the polynomialsrandt.
Our contribution.In this paper, we design the first multi-party protocolHQCde-
cryptionfor HQC.PKE decryption. This protocol is built around novel protocols :
ReedMuller-RandReedMuller-MLfor decoding duplicated Reed-Muller codewords as
well asReedSolomonfor Reed-Solomon codes, which are used in the design of HQC. Our
decryption protocol leads to the first threshold PKE based on HQC. This threshold PKE
achieves CPA security and relies on the exact same assumptions as the non-threshold HQC
cryptosystem in the UC framework. This MPC protocol is perfectly correct and does not
reveal any information about the decrypted message or the shared secret key. This security
property will be really crucial for designing a future CCA variant of this cryptosystem.
Our protocols are constant-rounds, meaning the number of rounds of interactions between
the participant is not dependent on the size of the shared inputs. As such, some of our
protocols are parametrized by a constantτ, unrelated to the shared inputs, that dictates
the number of rounds.
Building protocols for decoding Reed-Solomon and Reed-Muller codes is challenging
and requires new MPC techniques over fields of characteristic 2. To decode Reed-Muller
codewords, we propose two approaches. The first (ReedMuller-R), based on Reed’s
algorithm [Ree54], allows unique decoding as long as the number of errors is less than
half the code’s minimum distance. It requires the computation of the most common value
among a list of shared boolean values. To this end, we design a new majority computation
protocolMajorityusing techniques on shared polynomials from [GLOV24]. We believe

The second approach (ReedMuller-ML) fits the decoder of the HQC specification, as
it outputs a closest codeword even if the number of errors exceeds the previous bound.
It consists of exhaustively computing distances between candidate codewords as integers
encoded as an exponent of a public value. Comparisons and detection of a closest codeword
then rely on elementary MPC protocols.
Even if the first approach does not apply to the HQC cryptosystem, it is of independent
interest, since it is the first secure distributed variant of Reed algorithm, and its complexity
is actually asymptotically better than the second one.
The main difficulty in the secure distributed decoding of Reed-Solomon codes is to
compute the error locator polynomial without any information leakage. To this end,
we design the first multi-party protocolPadéfor solving a Padé approximant problem
corresponding to a secret polynomial whose coefficients are shared. We believe that this
protocol might be of independent interest and used in other settings. This protocol relies on
properties of subresultant matrices, which were already used in [MF06] for computing the
GCD of two secret polynomials. However, our solution is perfectly secure, in the sense that
participants learn absolutely no information about the secret polynomial, be it the degree or
values of the polynomials corresponding to the solution, or even the existence of a solution.
Our protocol’s main step boils down to computing the determinant of many subresultant
matrices. As such, we present a new protocolCharPolyfor computing characteristic
polynomials of matrices (which, in particular, allows for determinant computation) over
fields whose sizes are powers of two. The idea is inspired by the short discussion in [MV24]
where the authors suggest to adapt their protocol for the high-characteristic case to the
small characteristic one using techniques from Schönhage’s work in [Sch93]. We build on
this idea and pursue further its implications by designing all the necessary tools to achieve
a complete specification of this protocol. In particular, because Schönhage’s techniques
heavily rely on computing over rings of the formR = F2m[Y ]/Y d+1, we provide in
Section 3.1 the first MPC protocols dedicated to such operations. Although the techniques
employed are not novel, we believe that a comprehensive description of these new protocols,
along with complexity and security analysis, is of independent interest.
Our protocols rely exclusively on techniques that are independent of the underlying
MPC instantiation. As a result, they are agnostic to the specific MPC implementation
and can be readily adapted to a wide range of use cases.
Organization of the paperWe first lay some background notions in Section 2. In
Section 3, we detail the protocol for computing the characteristic polynomial of a shared
matrix in a field of characteristic 2, as well as the required protocols for handling computa-
tions for specific polynomial matrices and bivariate polynomials. In Section 4, we detail our
techniques for designing an MPC protocol for securely solving Padé approximants problems.
The protocol for Majority computation as well as the two Reed-Muller decoding protocols
are then detailed in Section 5. Our techniques for distributed Reed-Solomon decoding
are discussed in Section 6. Finally, we present our full protocol for HQC decryption in
Section 7, along with discussions regarding the sizes and efficiency of our new threshold
cryptosystem, and some concluding remarks for future work.

2.2 Security and base protocol functionalities
UCSecurityandhybridmodels.OurprotocolsareprovensecureusingtheUniversally
Composable (UC) framework introduced in [Can01]. It allows for proving the security
of our protocols, even when composed in other protocols and executed in parallel. This
framework is defined using Probabilistic Polynomial Time (PPT) Turing machines that
interact with other machines. Informally, this framework compares the behavior of an
environment Z interacting with an adversary either in a “real-world” experiment or an
“ideal-world” experiment. In the “real-world” experiment, the protocolΠforN parties is
executed. The environmentZ can choose the inputs of the parties and an adversaryA can
corrupt any subset of parties. The protocol is executed andA and Z can communicate
during the execution. At the end of the protocol,Z receives the output of all parties and
the adversary and outputs a decision bit. In the “ideal-world” experiment, the protocolΠ
is replaced by an ideal functionalityF. As before, Z can choose the input of the parties
and interact with an adversaryB. Then, it receives the outputs and returns a decision
bit. We say thatΠUC-realizes F if for any adversaryA in the “real-world”, there exists
a corresponding adversary B in the “ideal-world” such that any environmentZ has a
negligible advantage at differentiating between interacting withA in the “real-world” and
withBin the “ideal-world”.
In order to prove UC security for our protocol, we usually build a simulatorS that
simulates the views of the parties in an execution ofΠby interacting with the ideal
functionality F. This way, an adversary B in the “ideal-world” can be built for any
“real-world” adversaryA using the simulator, as the behavior ofA interacting withS and
in the protocolΠis the same.
Hybrid models introduced in [CLOS02] are useful in order to compose our protocols
securely in parallel. In theF-hybrid model, parties executing a protocolΠalso have an
unrestricted access to an ideal functionalityF. If a protocolP UC-realizes the functionality
F, let us denoteΠP the protocolΠwhere every call to F is replaced by an execution of
P. IfΠrealizes a functionalityGin theF-hybrid model, thenΠ P UC-realizesG.
The Arithmetic Black Box model.The MPC protocols presented here would typically
be instantiated using secret-sharing techniques. These methods allow a set of parties
to compute on secret data, composed of elements of a finite fieldFq, such that any set
of less thant participants cannot learn anything about said data, but a set of at least
t + 1participants can deduce the value of said data. While computing linear functions
of the shared data only requires local computation from the participants, computing a
sharing corresponding to the product of two previously shared secrets always requires
some form of communication. The first result to achieve such functionality is the BGW
protocol [BGW88] and nowadays many different secret-sharing techniques exist for different
security guarantees. In order to abstract from any choice of the underlying technique, we
present our protocols in theArithmetic Black-Box Model(ABB model). The ABB model
is standard in the literature and has been used in prior works to describe MPC protocol
with perfect security [HIKC23, CEN25]. In this model, parties have the ability to share
elements, reveal their values, and perform additions and multiplications. These are the
usual operations expected from secret-sharing techniques for MPC.

secret sharing allows to compute shares of a product[xy]from a linear combination of the
product of each players’ shares of[x]and[ y]. Shamir secret-sharing [Sha79], which is used
in the BGW protocol, is multiplicative. Moreover, any secret-sharing scheme that allows
for the base functionalities of the ABB model can be converted into a multiplicative secret
sharing scheme [CDM00].

4 Distributed Padé approximant solving
One of the crucial step while decoding Reed-Solomon codewords is finding the error-locator
polynomial. Using the syndrome of the erroneous codeword, one can find such a polynomial
by solving a Padé approximant problem. In the classical setting, the latter problem may
be solved using the Berlekamp-Massey algorithm [Mas69] or an adaptation of extended
euclidean algorithm. However, adapting these algorithms in the MPC setting is challenging,
as these methods often leak information about the data (for example, the steps of the
extended euclidean algorithm depends on the degree sequence of the input polynomials).
Moreover, they do not lead to constant-round protocols straightforwardly.
In this section, we present a novel constant-round MPC protocol for solving such
Padé approximant problems. As our protocol makes use of linear algebra, it relies on
properties of subresultants and Padé approximants. We introduce the required background
in section 4.1. Then, we give a detailed description of the protocol as well as the techniques
used in section 4.2

5 Distributed Reed-Muller decoding
In this section, we present MPC protocols for decoding shared noisy first-order duplicated
Reed-Muller codewords. This type of code is used for the inner code in the final proposition
for HQC.PKE. Therefore, these protocols will be used as subprotocols for our shared
decryption of an HQC ciphertext.
The decoder of the HQC implementation (as well as the one of [MS77]) uses a fast
Hadamard transform in order to find a closest codeword to the input. We first consider the
original decoding algorithm by Reed [Ree54], which relies on majority votes and recovers
the transmitted codeword as long as the number of errors is less than half the minimum
distance of the code. We introduce this algorithm, and then present our novel MPC
techniques to design the corresponding MPC protocol, namely a subprotocol to securely
compute the majority among a set of shared values. In Section 5.4, we present a second
MPC protocol, which securely computes a closest codeword to the input, and therefore
exactly matches the decoder of the HQC implementation (and hence its decryption failure
rate). We believe that both MPC protocols remains of interest outside the thresholdization
of HQC and thus motivate their detailed presentation.

5.4 Closest codeword decoding
Algorithm 9 is guaranteed to recover the transmitted codeword as long as there are fewer
than γ2m−2errors. The decoder of the HQC specification [AAB+22] instead outputs
a closest codeword to its input, which may succeed well beyond this bound, and the
decryption failure rate analysis of HQC relies on this decoder. In this section, we present
an MPC protocol that computes exactly such a closest codeword, in a constant number
of rounds (independent ofm and γ). Since Hamming distances are integers, they cannot
be directly computed on values shared in a field of characteristic2. Instead, we compute
the distance between the input and each candidate codewordin the exponentof a public
field elementρ, using products of non-zero elements. Two distances are then compared by
evaluating a public bivariate polynomial on the corresponding powers ofρ, which amounts
to a single inner product and directly outputs the result of the comparison in the exponent.
Finally, the position of the minimum is obtained with an unboundedAND of these results,
computed as a product.

determinants computed in protocolPadé.
Comparison with [GLOV26].
While the main design principle behind both cryptosystems (i.e. designing a MPC protocol
for decryption) is the same, their comparison is not straightforward. First, the schemes
from [GLOV26] achieve a different security notion, so we compare only to the IND-CPA
variant (i.e. before applying their generic conversion from IND-CPA to IND-CCA security).
Moreover, as explained below, the constraints on the underlying MPC field are not the
same. Thus, the communication induced by a given number of secure multiplications might
be different.
We compare threshold PKEs based on both McEliece and HQC, using parameter sets
for NIST security levels (1, 3 and 5), on two main aspects: the size of the different elements
of the cryptosystem itself (i.e. ciphertext size, public and partial secret key size), and
communication estimates for the MPC protocols (i.e. size of the field, number of secure
multiplications, number of rounds).
For [GLOV26], the dominant constraint on the base field for MPC is that it needs to
contain the field corresponding to the Goppa code construction, which means that this
field can be picked for the MPC protocol.

In terms of security comparison, two aspects need to be considered: the security related
to the decryption protocols, and the security of the base schemes. Our decryption protocols
as well as the one in [GLOV26] are built in the ABB model. Therefore, in order to
compare the assumptions required for the schemes, we would have to compare the security
assumptions and guarantees that would be needed for the MPC protocols, which depend on
the chosen instantiation of the protocols. Furthermore, thresholdization does not add any
new assumption compared to the respective base schemes, nor modify the parameter sets

Efficiency improvements.One could circumvent the possible higher cost of threshold
HQC in several ways. First, one could tweak the parameters sets of HQC to find a
better balance between Reed-Muller and Reed-Solomon decoding (i.e. allowing for greater
correction capacity for Reed-Solomon when the cost of Reed-Muller is dominant, and vice-
versa). The security of these new parameters would need to be thoroughly analyzed. Next,
one could consider using protocols that have a non-constant number of round in theory,
but might have better communication costs in practice when applied to HQC decryption.
This would probably lead to very different MPC techniques than those presented in this
article.
CCA security.Achieving CCA security would also be desirable, but is highly non trivial
and could be the topic of future work. This could be achieved by applying some kind
of variants of either the Fujisaki-Okamoto transform [FO13, HHK17] or the Naor-Yung
transform [NY90, Lin03], adapted to the threshold context. Both solutions would have
challenges to overcome. On one hand, a FO-like transform would require evaluating hash
functions, and a re-encryption step in MPC, which would degrade the efficiency of the
threshold decryption protocol even more. On the other hand, a Naor-Yung-style transform
(the route [GLOV26] took) would have two problems to solve. First, it would require the
design of a Zero-Knowledge proof system tailored to well-formed HQC ciphertexts, which
does not currently exist and is non-trivial to build because of the quasi-cyclic properties of
the scheme. Second, HQC has a non-zero decryption failure rate, which would have to be
folded into the analysis of any such transform.
