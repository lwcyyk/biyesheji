Randomizing Polynomials: A New Representation 
with Applications to Round-Efficient Secure Computation 
(Extended Abstract) 
Yuval Ishai * 
Abstract 
Motivated by questions about secure multi-party compu- 
tation, we introduce and study a new natural representa- 
tion offunctions by polynomials, which we term randomiz- 
ing polynomials. “Standard” low-degree polynomials over 
ajnitejeldare easy to compute witha small numberof com- 
munication rounds in virtually any setting for secure com- 
putation. Howevel; most Boolean functions cannot be eval- 
uated by a polynomial whose degree is smaller than their in- 
put size. We get around this barrier by relaxing the require- 
ment of evaluating f into a weaker requirement of randomiz- 
ing f: mapping the inputs off along with independent ran- 
dom inputs into a vector of outputs, whose distribution de- 
pends only on the value off. We show that degree-3 poly- 
nomials are sufficient to randomize any function f, relating 
the efficiency of such a randomization to the branching pro- 
gram size of f. On the other hand, by characterizing the ex- 
act class of Boolean functions which can be randomized by 
degree-2 polynomials, we show that 3 is the minimal ran- 
domization degree of most functions. 
As an application, randomizing polynomials provide a 
powerjiul, general, and conceptually simple tool for the de- 
sign of round-efficient secure protocols. Specifically, the se- 
cure evaluation of any function can be reduced to a secure 
evaluation of degree-3 polynomials. One corollary of this 
reduction is that two (respectively, three) communication 
rounds are sufficient for k parties to compute any Boolean 
function f of their inputs, with perfect information-theoretic 
ly J -privacy (resp., [YJ -privacy), and communication 
complexity which is at most quadratic in the branchingpm- 
gram size off (with a small probability of one-sided error).

1.1. Related Work 
Different notions of representation of Boolean functions 
by polynomials (and, in particular, by low-degree polyno- 
mials) have been applied in numerous complexity theoretic 
contexts. For instance, polynomial representations have 
been applied in the areas of circuit lower-bounds, interac- 
tive proofs and PCP characterizations of NP, average-case 
to worst-case reductions for hard probIems, and program 
checking (see [ 191 for a survey of some related work). The 
reader is referred to [28,3 1,24,32,3,33,26,7] for results on 
polynomial representations and their applications in com- 
plexity theory. Our notion of randomizing polynomials may 
be viewed as another natural form of polynomial represen- 
tation, with a different flavor of applications. 
The round complexity of interactive protocols is one of 
their most important complexity measures. Consequently, a 
substantial amount of research effort has been invested into 
characterizing the exact round complexity of various tasks 
(such as zero-knowledge proofs, Byzantine Agreement, and 
others). In particular, the problem of obtaining constant- 
round secure computation protocols has attracted a consid- 
erable amount of attention [2,6,5, 17,22,29,4, 10, 141. In 
the following we briefly survey some relevant works, start- 
ing with the information-theoretic setting. 
The problem was first explicitly considered by Bar-Ilan 
and Beaver [2]. They constructed an efficient, (expected) 
constant-round, information-theoretically secure protocol 
with an optimal security threshold for every function in 
NC1. Feige, Kilian, and Naor [17], followed by Ishai 
and Kushilevitz [22], study a specialized non-interactive 
model for secure computation. Their results can be used to 
extend the class of functions for which efficient constant- 
round protocols are known from NC’ to NL and similar log- 
space classes. A slight further extension of this class, as 
well as communication-efficient constant-round protocols 
for natural linear algebra problems, are given by Cramer and 
Damghd [14]. 
A key idea in the constructions of [2, 171, originally due 
to Kilian [23], is a reduction of the evaluation of f to an 
evaluation of an iterated product in a finite group. The tech- 
nique which was used for a constant-round secure evalua- 
tion of this product, in turn, required a distributed generation 
of secret group elements coupled with their inverses. This 
was realized in [2] by using an interactive inversion sub- 
protocol, whose output was subsequently used by the main 
protocol (incurring additional rounds of interaction). In the 
current work we utilize a different, inversion-free, random- 
ization approach, extending a technique from [22]. An alter- 
native inversion-free randomization technique (though gen- 
erally less efficient than that of [22]) was recently suggested 
by Beaver [4]. While protocols from [22, 41 still require a 
distributed generation of secret invertible matrices (realized

2.2. Secure Multi-Party Computation 
Let PI, . . . , Pk be k parties, where each party Pj holds 
an input yj. In this paper, each yj will either be a bit string or 
a vector over the field K. We will use y to denote the k-tuple 
of inputs, and z = (~1,. . . , z,) to denote the concatena- 
tion of the k inputs, viewed as a vector from K?. The parties 
wish to compute some function f on their inputs. For sim- 
plicity, we will only consider here the case of deterministic, 
single-output functions. 
To the end of computing f, the parties engage in a ran- 
domized distributed protocol. Initially, each party Pj holds 
its input yj and an independent random input. Aprotocol3 
proceeds by synchronous rounds, where at each round each 
party may send messages to all otherparties depending on its 
input, its random input, and the messages it received in pre- 
vious rounds. At the end of each round, each party Pj may 
decide to terminate and output some value zj. The execution 
terminates when all parties terminate. The round complex- 
ity of 3 is defined as the maximal number of rounds before 
termination, taken over all possible executions of F. 
There are two major settings for secure computation: (a) 
The secure channels model [9, 12,27, 161, where each pair 
of parties may secretly communicate with each other. In this 
model security is usually required to be information theo- 
retic. (b) The computational model [35, 211, where no se- 
cure channels are assumed but instead parties are restricted 
to probabilistic polynomial-time computations; as a result 
intractability assumptions can be made and the notion of se- 
curity becomes computational. Intuitively, a protocol is said 
to be t-secure, in any of the settings, if it is resilient to an ad- 
versary which is allowed to corrupt at most t parties. This 
is formalized by requiring that whatever an adversary can 
achieve (and learn) in the “real-life” execution of the pro- 
tocol, it could have also achieved in an ideal model, where a 
trusted party is being used to perform the computation. The 
different notions of security differ in the amount of “cormp- 
tion power” the adversary may have. An active adversary 
is allowed to maliciously alter the behavior of the parties it 
corrupts, whereas a passive adversary only learns their view 
of the protocol. 
In the following we define the simplest variant of 
information-theoretic security against a passive adversary. 
Security in this setting will be referred to as privacy. We say 
that 3 is an E-correct, (perfectly) t-private protocol for com- 
puting f, if it satisfies the following two properties: (1) E- 
correctness - for every input y, the probability (over the ran- 
dom inputs) that some party fails to output f(z) is bounded 
by E; in the current work we mostly consider this probabilis- 
tic notion of correctness (where E may be taken to be expo- 
nentially small), rather than the perfect one. (2) t-privacy - a 
collusion of at most t parties cannot learn any information 
about the inputs of the remaining parties, other than what 
follows from their inputs and the value off. This can be 
formalized by requiring the existence of a probabilistic sim- 
ulator algorithm S, satisfying the following condition. For 
any input y and collusion B of at most t parties, the output 
generated by S on input (B, YE, f(y)), where YB denotes 
y restricted to its B-entries, is distributed identically to the 
joint view of parties from B in the execution of 3 on input y 
(including the inputs, random inputs, and communication). 
Finally, iff is a Boolean function, we say that 3 is €-correct 
with a one-sided error if 3 is +correct and it never errs on 
input y such that f(y) = 0. 
The exact definition of security in more general or more 
adversarial settings is a delicate issue; for a more complete 
treatment the reader is referred to, e.g., [ 11,20,25]. We em- 
phasize though that the issues addressed in this paper are 
quite insensitive to the exact notion of security. Our results 
may be viewed as providing an information-theoretic reduc- 
tion from the problem of securely computing a function f to 
the problem of securely computing a vector of low-degree 
polynomials.

2.3. Branching Programs 
Our constructions use the following notion of modular 
branching programs, which generalizes the standard no- 
tions of deterministic and non-deterministic branching pro- 
grams. 
Definition 2.5 A (non-deterministic} mod-p branching pro- 
gram is dejined by a quadruple BP = (G, 4, s, t), where 
G = (V, E) is a directed acyclic graph, 4 is a labeling 
function assigning each edge a negative literal x:, a posi- 
tive literal xi or the constant 1, and s, t are two special ver- 
tices. Each input assignment x = (XI,. . . , x,) naturally 
induces an unlabeledsubgraph G,, whose edges include ev- 
ery e E E such that $(e) is satisjied by x. The function com- 
puted by BP is the Boolean function f satisfying f (x) = 1 
iff the number of “accepting” s - t paths in G, is nonzero 
modulo-p. Finally, dejine the size of BP to be [VI, the num- 
ber of vertices in G. 
The better known model of deterministic branching pro- 
grams may be defined as the special case in which for every 
input x, the out-degree of every vertex in G, is at most 1 
(note that in this case, the choice of p does not make a differ- 
ence, and it can be fixed to be as small as 2). A definition of 
standard non-deterministic branching programs may be ob- 
tained from the above by counting the number of accepting 
paths over the integers, rather than modulo p. Thus, when p 
is larger than the possible number of paths, mod-p computa- 
tion and non-deterministiccomputation coincide (see [34,8] 
for better simulations of non-deterministic branching pro- 
grams by modular branching programs). 
In contrast to constant-degree polynomials, branching 
programs are quite powerful. In particular, every function 
can be computed by a branching program, and many “nat- 
ural” function families (in particular regular languages) by 
linear-size branching programs. The branching program 
size of any function f is no larger than the Boolean for- 
mula size of f: However, as the power of (uniformly con- 
structible) polynomial-size branching programs coincides 
with that of different variants of log-space computation, 
they are probably not powerful enough to efficiently com- 
pute all polynomial-time computable functions.

6. Concluding Remarks 
Randomizing polynomials introduce a simple and pow- 
erful paradigm for the design of round-efficient secure 
computation protocols. A long-standing open question is 
whether every polynomial-time computable function can 
be computed by an eflcient constant-round protocol with 
information-theoretic privacy and linear privacy threshold 
[6, 2, 51. This question is open even in a setting where 
the parties may be computationally unbounded. While gen- 
eral interactive protocols are difficult to analyze, there seems 
to be much better hope for characterizing the power of 
the more restricted model of randomizing polynomials, for 
example by relating it to well-studied complexity models. 
Gaining a better understanding of randomizing polynomials 
may be a first step towards resolving the above question. 
An open question of a somewhat different flavor is that 
of finding the exact number of rounds required for privately 
evaluating an arbitrary (i.e., a worst-case) function f with an 
optimal privacy threshold. Using randomizing polynomials, 
an upper bound of 3 was obtained. If this bound is tight (i.e., 
2 rounds are not enough) then, in a very crude sense, the ran- 
domizing polynomials approach is non-restrictive. 
Finally, there are several natural variants of the random- 
izing polynomials model which were not studied in this 
work. These include a statistically-private relaxation and a 
perfectly-correct restriction of the model.
